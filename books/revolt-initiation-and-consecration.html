Initiation and Consecration

“What emerges in this context is a situation in which there is a certain distance between the person and the function being exercised: in order for a person to embody a certain function what is required is a specific action capable of producing in him a new quality; this action may appear either in the form of an initiation or of an investiture (or consecration). In the first case this action has a relatively autonomous and direct character; in the second case it is mediated, or it takes place from the outside through a priestly caste distinct from the regal caste.” Revolt Against the Modern World

In the primordial form of Tradition the regal quality is not acquired. The king is a divine being or carries an Olympic nature by birth. The older languages mark the difference with precision. A *deus* is an eternal divine being. A *divus* or a hero is a mortal who, at some point in his existence, becomes a god. The first case knows no gap between the person and the function. The second case already admits a distance. That distance is the first sign that the race of the spirit has begun to weaken. When the quality is no longer innate, something must be done to produce it.

The action that produces the new quality takes one of two forms. The first is initiation. It is relatively autonomous and direct. The man himself undergoes a real transformation of being. The second is investiture or consecration. It is mediated from outside by a priestly caste distinct from the regal caste. The distinction is not ceremonial. It is ontological. Initiation changes the structure of the being who receives it. Consecration confers a rank and a set of powers upon a being who remains, in substance, what he was. The older traditions still knew the first. Later forms retained only the second and called the result sacred.

Regal initiation is the reactivation of a potential that is already latent. It does not create the higher nature out of nothing. It removes the obstacles that keep that nature from becoming effective. The rites therefore follow a fixed sequence. First comes purification: the lower elements of the body and the psyche are stripped or subordinated. Then come the trials. Isolation, fasting, physical hardship, and symbolic death prepare the ground. The candidate crosses a boundary that ordinary life does not cross. In many traditions the boundary is represented as a river or a stretch of water. To pass it is to leave the domain of ordinary becoming and to enter a different order. The ascent of a mountain or the entry into a sealed chamber completes the same passage. What returns is no longer the man who entered.

The Egyptian enthronement rites show the pattern with particular clarity. The king is assimilated to a divine figure through a sequence of acts that reenact a mythological drama. He is not merely crowned. He is made to participate in the powers and the mode of being of the god. Parallel forms appear in the Roman and Hellenic traditions. The *divus* is the man who has undergone the change. The same logic governs the higher mysteries. The Eleusinian sequence of crossing, purification, and vision is one instance. The point is always the same: a new quality of being is actualized. The man who completes the process stands in a different relation to the forces of the world. He is no longer merely subject to them. He can act as their mediator or as their master.

When even this capacity is lost, the tradition retains only the external form. Consecration by a priestly caste becomes the normal procedure. The king receives an investiture. Titles, insignia, and ritual formulas are conferred upon him. The act may still carry a residual force, yet the decisive change of being no longer occurs inside the king himself. The quality is mediated. The priestly caste, which in the primordial order was subordinate to the regal function, now appears as the necessary intermediary. This is a second and deeper degeneration. The bridge between the two natures is no longer built by the man who must cross it. It is administered by others. The pontifex, the bridge-builder, ceases to be the king and becomes a separate specialist.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. Authority that rests only on a title, an election, or a rite performed by others is already a reduced form. The older measure required an interior change: the actualization of a higher nature that is either innate or recovered through initiation. Without that change the function remains external. The man who holds the office and the quality the office is supposed to embody stay separate. In a late age the separation is almost total. The recognition of that fact is the first condition for understanding both the traditional order and the forces that dissolved it.
