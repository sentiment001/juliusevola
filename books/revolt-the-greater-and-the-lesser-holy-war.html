The Greater and the Lesser Holy War

“In the traditional vision every reality was a symbol and every action a rite, so this could not fail to hold for war as well. Thus war could assume a sacred character, and ‘holy war’ and the ‘way of God’ could become one and the same thing.” Revolt Against the Modern World

In the traditional vision every reality is a symbol and every action a rite. War could not escape this law. It could assume a sacred character, and holy war and the way of God become one and the same. More or less explicitly this conception appears in many traditions. A religious aspect and a transcendent intention attached themselves to the bloody and conquering enterprises of traditional humanity. Livy notes that the Samnite warriors presented the aspect of initiates. Residual echoes of the same idea appear among savage populations in the form of warrior initiations and mixtures of the warrior element with the magical. In ancient Mexico the rank of commander, tecuhtli, required the successful passage of hard tests of initiatory type. The Japanese warrior nobility of the Samurai was animated in part by the doctrines and ascesis of Zen, an esoteric form of Buddhism.

The ancient vision of the world and the myth already favored, by the frequency of the theme of antagonism, the spiritual integration of the idea of combat. The Iranian-Aryan conception has already been indicated. In the ancient Hellenic world the material struggle was frequently seen as the reflection of the eternal cosmic struggle between the uranian-Olympian spiritual element of the cosmos and the titanic, demonic-feminine or unrestrained elementary element of chaos. This meaning appeared especially where war was associated with the idea of Empire, because of the transcendent significance that, as already shown, belonged to that idea and that translated itself into an idea-force of particular intensity. The symbolism of the struggles of Heracles, the hero allied to the Olympian forces, was applied to Frederick I Hohenstaufen in his struggle for the defense of imperial right.

Special conceptions relative to the destinies of the afterlife give the key to the interior meanings of warrior ascesis. For the Aztec and Nahua races the highest place of immortality, the House of the Sun or of Uitzilopochtli, was reserved not only to sovereigns but also to heroes. For the others one conceived at most a slow extinction in a place analogous to the Hellenic Hades. The Nordic-Aryan conception of the Walhalla is the place of celestial immortality destined, beyond the nobles and free men of divine stock, to the heroes fallen on the battlefield. The lord of this place, linked to the symbolism of altitude and often identified with Asgard itself, the residence of the Aesir situated in the Middle Land, is Odin-Wotan, the Nordic god of war and victory. In one version of the myth this king, by his own sacrifice, showed the heroes the way that leads to the divine dwelling where one lives eternally and where they become his sons. For the Nordic-Aryans no sacrifice or cult was more desired by the supreme god and richer in supernatural fruits than that celebrated by the hero who falls on the battlefield. From the declaration of war until the bloody victory the religious element impregnated the Germanic ranks and carried the individual as well. With his heroic death the warrior passes from the plane of material terrestrial war to that of a struggle of universal and transcendent character. The forces of the heroes feed the so-called Wildes Heer, the stormy horde of which Odin is the chief, which launches from the summit of Mount Walhalla and then returns to rest. In the higher forms of this tradition the horde of heroes gathered by the Valkyries for Odin on the battlefields, with which the Wildes Heer ends by being confused, is the phalanx of which the god has need to fight against the ragna-rökkr, against the destiny that darkens the divine, comparable to the kali-yuga or dark age of the Hindu tradition, that hangs over the world from remote ages. However great the number of heroes gathered in the Walhalla, they will always be few when the wolf comes. This is the general conception of the integration of war into holy war.

In the Islamic tradition two holy wars are distinguished. One is the greater holy war, el-jihâdul-akbar. The other is the lesser holy war, el-jihâdul-açghar. The distinction comes from a saying of the Prophet who, returning from a warlike expedition, said: We have returned from the lesser holy war to the greater holy war. The greater war is of internal and spiritual order. The other is the material war fought externally against an enemy people, in particular with the intention of recovering infidel peoples for the space in which the law of God, dar al-islâm, prevails. Yet the greater holy war stands to the lesser as the soul stands to the body. For the understanding of the heroic ascesis or way of action it is fundamental to grasp the situation in which the two become one, the lesser holy war becoming the means by which a greater holy war is realized and vice versa. The lesser, the exterior, becomes almost a ritual action that expresses and bears witness to the reality of the first. In its origins orthodox Islam conceived a single form of ascesis: that which is linked precisely to the jihad, to the holy war. The greater holy war is the struggle of man against the enemies he carries within himself. More exactly, it is the struggle of the highest principle of man against everything that is merely human in him, against his inferior nature and what is disordered impulse and material longing. This is given in explicit terms in a text of Aryan warrior wisdom, the Bhagavad-gîtâ: Realizing what is found beyond reason, strengthening yourself by yourself, kill the enemy that presents itself under the form of desire hard to conquer. It is as cupidity and animal instinct, as disordered multiplicity of impulses, as anxious limitation of the fictitious I, and therefore also as fear, weakness and uncertainty, that the enemy that resists, the infidel within us, must be struck down and reduced to slavery. This is the condition for attaining inner liberation, the rebirth in a deep unity with oneself. In the Western traditions of the Ars Regia this is expressed equally by the symbolism of the two enemies that become one, as well as by the peace in the esoteric and triumphal sense already indicated.

In the world of traditional warrior ascesis the lesser holy war, the exterior war, is added or even prescribed as a way to realize this greater holy war. For this reason in Islam holy war, jihad, and way of Allah are frequently used as synonyms. In this order of ideas the action has rigorously the function and the purpose of a sacrificial and purifying rite. Thus it is written in the Quran: They fight in the way of God, that is in the holy war, those who sacrifice the earthly life to that of the future: for to him who fights in the way of God and is killed or victorious, we will give a great reward. The presupposition from which it is prescribed is: Fight in the way of God those who make war on you. Kill them wherever you find them and repel them. Do not show yourselves weak nor propose peace. When you encounter those who do not believe, strike them until you make a carnage. In the case of a true death in war one therefore has the equivalent of the mors triumphalis of which one speaks in the classical traditions. He who, in the lesser war, has lived the greater holy war, will have awakened a force that will verisimilarly make him overcome the crisis of death and that, after having already liberated him from the enemy and the infidel, will make him escape the destiny of the Hades. The promise is the same: Dead, you will gain paradise; victorious, you will possess the earth: therefore rise resolute for the combat. The interior orientation, the Islamic niyyah, capable of transforming the lesser war into a greater holy war, is declared in very clear terms. Dedicating all works to me, says the god Krishna, with your spirit fixed on the supreme state of the Self, free from every idea of possession, free from fever in the spirit, fight. It is in equally clear terms that one speaks of the purity of this action, which must be willed for itself: Considering as equal pleasure and pain, gain and loss, victory and defeat, prepare yourself for the battle: thus you will not have any impurity. By this way is represented the identification of war with the way of God. The warrior evokes in himself the transcendent force of destruction, assumes it, transfigures himself in it and liberates himself, breaking the human bond. Life is like a bow; the soul like a dart; the target to be pierced the Supreme Spirit: to join oneself to it, as the shot arrow fixes itself in the target, says another text of the same tradition. This is the metaphysical justification of war, the assumption of the lesser war into greater holy war.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. War is not a merely material event. When every reality is a symbol and every action a rite, the lesser war can become the ritual vehicle of the greater. The enemy outside is the occasion for striking the enemy inside: desire, fear, the fictitious I, the disordered impulses. The orientation that equalizes victory and defeat, gain and loss, and that fixes the spirit on the supreme state of the Self, turns the action into a sacrificial rite. Death on that path is not extinction. It is the mors triumphalis that awakens a force capable of overcoming the crisis of death and of escaping the destiny of the Hades. To refuse this possibility is to leave war as pure destruction or as collective passion. To accept it is to recover the condition under which the warrior can pass from the terrestrial plane to the plane of a struggle of universal character, feeding the forces that stand against the darkening of the divine. The recognition that the greater holy war stands to the lesser as the soul stands to the body is the condition for seeing what the traditional world still knew about action under a higher measure and why that knowledge has been driven out of the modern age.
