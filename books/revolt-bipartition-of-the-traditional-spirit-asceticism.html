Bipartition of the Traditional Spirit; Asceticism

“After the meaning of the castes has been indicated, it is necessary to refer to that way which, in a certain sense, stands above the castes and responds to the impulse toward the direct realization of transcendence, in terms analogous to those of high initiation, yet outside the specific and rigorous structures of the latter.” Revolt Against the Modern World

After the meaning of the castes has been indicated, a further way must be recognized. It stands, in a certain sense, above the castes. It answers the impulse toward the direct realization of transcendence in terms analogous to those of high initiation, yet it operates outside the specific and rigorous structures of initiation itself. The pariah is the sub-caste, the fallen one. He is the man who has escaped form because he proved impotent before it, and who therefore returns to the infernal world. The ascetic is the supra-caste. He detaches himself from form by renouncing the illusory center of human individuality. He does not do so through fidelity to his own nature and hierarchical participation. He does so by means of a direct action that turns toward the principle from which every form proceeds. In the Aryan India the repulsion felt by every caste for the pariah was equaled only by the veneration shown by all for the supra-caste. According to a Buddhist image, one demands of him as little obedience to a human dharma as the man who seeks fire concerns himself with the species of wood that, once burned, will produce flame and light in equal measure. Ascesis therefore occupies an ideal intermediate place between the plane of direct Olympian, regal, and initiatory superiority and the plane of the rite and of dharma.

Ascesis further presents two aspects. These may be regarded as the two qualifications of the traditional spirit itself. The first is action as heroic action. The second is ascesis in the strict sense, referring above all to the way of contemplation. Beyond the complete forms of Tradition, later civilizations developed that were oriented more or less toward one or the other of these two poles. The part each orientation has played in the dynamism of historical forces, even on a plane not without relation to the ethnic and racial factor, will be seen in its place. Action and knowledge are the two fundamental faculties of man. In the order of each an integration is possible that removes the human limit. The ascesis of contemplation is precisely the integration of the cognitive virtue. It is obtained by detachment from sensible reality, by the neutralization of the individual ratiocinative faculties, and by the progressive denudation of the nucleus of consciousness. That nucleus deconditions itself. It subtracts itself from limitation and from the necessity of every determination, whether real or virtual. Once every residue and every obstruction has been eliminated, the opus remotionis is complete. Participation in the supra-world is realized under the species of vision or of illumination. This summit of the ascetic approximation is at the same time the beginning of a truly constant, progressive ascent that realizes states of being superior to the human condition. The universal as knowledge, and knowledge as liberation, are the essential ideals of the ascetic way.

The ascetic detachment proper to the contemplative way implies renunciation. On this point the misunderstanding fostered by inferior forms of ascesis must be prevented. The sense of renunciation in the high ascesis of antiquity and of the East differs from the sense it has acquired in a large part of religious ascesis, especially Christian. In the latter case renunciation has often presented the character of a repression and a mortification. The Christian ascetic detaches himself from the objects of desire not because he no longer desires them, but in order to mortify himself and to withdraw from temptation. In the other case renunciation proceeds from a natural disgust for the objects that commonly attract and that are desired. It proceeds from the fact that one feels directly that one desires, or more precisely that one wills, something the world of conditioned existence cannot give. A natural nobility of one’s own desire therefore leads to renunciation. It is not an exterior intervention intended to restrain, mortify, and inhibit the faculty of desire belonging to a vulgar nature. The affective moment, even in its hardest and noblest forms, figures only in the first degrees of high ascesis. Thereafter it is consumed by the intellectual fire and by the arid splendor of pure contemplation.

Early Buddhism may be indicated as the typical example of contemplative ascesis. It is free of religious elements and is organized as a pure system of techniques. The spirit that permeates it is distant from everything one is now led to think of when speaking of asceticism. Buddhism knows no gods in the religious sense. The gods appear to it as powers that are themselves in need of liberation, so that the Awakened One, as he is superior to men, is also superior to the gods. The ascetic, it is said in the canon, frees himself not only from the human bond but also from the divine. Moral norms appear in the original forms of Buddhism only as instruments in the service of the objective realization of supra-individual states. Everything that belongs to the world of believing and of faith, and that is bound to affective moments, is avoided. The fundamental principle of the method is knowledge. Knowledge of the non-identity of the Self with any other, even if that other be the all or the world of Brahma the theistic god, is made into a fire that progressively destroys every irrational identification with what is conditioned. The point of arrival, beyond the negative designation (nirvana equals cessation of the agitation), is itself given, in conformity with the way, in terms of knowledge: bodhi, knowledge in the eminent sense, supra-rational illumination, knowledge that liberates as in an awakening from a sleep, from a stupor, from an hallucination. This does not equate to the cessation of force or to something like a dissolution. To dissolve bonds is not to dissolve. It is to liberate. The image of the one who, free from every yoke whether divine or human, is supremely autonomous and can go where he wills, is most recurrent in the Buddhist canon. It is accompanied by every kind of symbol of a virile and warrior character, and by the constant and explicit reference not to non-being but to something superior both to being and to non-being. The Buddha belonged to an ancient stock of the Aryan warrior nobility. His doctrine, presenting itself as the doctrine of the nobles inaccessible to the vulgar, is far from every mystic evasion. It is permeated by a sense of superiority, of clarity, and of spiritual indomitability. Free, knower, proud, sovereign, of whom neither the gods nor the spirits nor men know the way, the Supreme, the hero, the great victorious seer, the impassible, the Lord of rebirth: thus the Awakened One is called in the original texts. Buddhist renunciation is of the virile and aristocratic type already indicated. It is dictated by force. It is not imposed by need. It is willed for the overcoming of need and for the reintegration of a perfect life.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. There is a way that stands above the castes. It is the direct turning toward the principle from which every form proceeds, accomplished by the renunciation of the illusory center of the human individuality. The pariah falls below form by impotence. The supra-caste rises above form by a natural nobility of desire that no longer finds its object in the conditioned world. Ascesis occupies the intermediate place between direct Olympian superiority and the plane of rite and dharma, and it divides according to the two poles of the traditional spirit: heroic action and contemplative ascesis. In the pure contemplative form the method is knowledge that destroys every irrational identification with the conditioned. Early Buddhism supplies the type: no religious gods, no affective faith, nirvana as the extinction of the mania rather than the extinction of force, and a doctrine of the nobles marked by clarity, autonomy, and warrior symbols. To confuse this renunciation with mortification is to invert the order. To recognize it is to recover the condition under which the differentiated man can still turn toward the principle that precedes every form, even when the hierarchical structures that once supported him have dissolved.
