Polar Symbolism; the Lord of Peace and Justice

“The polar symbolism is connected with the idea of a motionless center that organizes and dominates the forces of becoming. The cakravartin is the Lord of the Wheel, the Lord of Peace and of Justice.” — Julius Evola, Revolt Against the Modern World

Polar symbolism names the function of the fixed center. In the traditional order the king occupies the place of the pole. He is the immovable point around which the entire structure turns. Solar symbolism shows the generative light that overcomes darkness by its own nature. Polar symbolism shows the stability that remains unaffected by the stream of becoming. The two belong together. Without the fixed center the light has no axis. Without the light the center has no generative force. The traditional king holds both. His presence gives the lower world a true center that is not of the lower world.

The cakravartin is the figure that concentrates this function. The word means the one who turns the wheel. In the Hindu tradition he is the universal monarch who sets the wheel of the law in motion while remaining at the hub. He is dharmaraja, the Lord of the Law, and the Lord of Peace and Justice. The wheel represents the ordered movement of the forces of the lower nature under a higher measure. The hub is the point that does not move. When the king holds the polar place the energies of the society are organized and subjected to the higher principle. When that place is vacant the wheel continues to turn but it turns without a center. The result is movement without direction and activity without form.

Peace is the attribute of this stability. It is not the absence of conflict or the cessation of activity. It is the inner condition of the one who has overcome the lower nature in himself and therefore can serve as the fixed point for others. The traditional texts speak of the king as a lighthouse of peace in the storm. He does not eliminate the storm. He remains the calm center that organizes its forces. Justice is the concrete ordering that flows from this center. It is the application of the higher measure to the particular conditions of the lower world. Peace and justice are not political goals in the modern sense. They are the visible signs that the polar function is present and that the order is oriented upward.

These attributes appear across traditional civilizations. In the West the title imperator pacificus was given to the one who embodied this function. Melchizedek, king of Salem, appears as one of the many representations of the universal king who is both priest and king in the higher sense, the Lord of Peace and of Justice. The traditional world knew that any claim to rule must carry this quality or it remains closed in the domain of becoming. The modern world has replaced the polar center with a succession of shifting forces. Numbers, interests, technical competence, and ideological programs take turns at the center. None of them can supply the fixed point. The result is continuous movement without form and administration without order.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is the recognition that a true order requires a fixed center that is not of the becoming. The polar function is not an archaic political form. It is the concrete proof that a man can stand as the immovable axis and thereby give the lower world a true orientation. To hold this idea is to measure every claim to rule and every structure of society by whether it possesses or lacks this center. The absence of the polar function is the decisive fact of the age. Without it the structures of a civilization remain closed in the domain of becoming, and the revolt against the modern world has no concrete political ground.
