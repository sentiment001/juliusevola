The Decline of the Medieval Ecumen and the Birth of Nations

“Once an empire ceases to be sacred, it begins to cease being an Empire. Its principle and its authority drop in level and, once the plane of matter and of simple politics is reached, they cannot maintain themselves, because this plane, by its very nature, excludes every universality and every superior unity.” Revolt Against the Modern World

Causes from above and causes from below produce the decadence of the Holy Roman Empire and of the principle of true sovereignty. Among the first stands the progressive secularization and materialization of the political idea. Already under Frederick II the struggle against the Church, undertaken to defend the supernatural character of the Empire, shows the beginning of this evolution: humanism, liberalism and nascent rationalism of the Sicilian court, the constitution of a body of lay judges and administrative employees, the importance taken by the legistae and the decretistae and by those whom a just religious rigorism, lighting auto-da-fés and Savonarolan stakes for the first products of “culture” and of “free thought,” qualified with contempt as theologi philosophantes. On the other side appears the centralizing and already anti-feudal tendency of certain new imperial institutions. At the moment an empire ceases to be sacred it begins to cease being an Empire. Its principle and its authority drop in level and, once the plane of matter and of simple politics is reached, they cannot maintain themselves, because this plane by its very nature excludes every universality and every superior unity. In 1338 already Louis IV of Bavaria declares that imperial consecration is no longer necessary and that the elected prince is legitimate emperor by virtue of that election alone: emancipation that Charles IV of Bohemia completes with the Golden Bull. Because the consecration was replaced by nothing metaphysically equivalent, the emperors destroyed in this way their own transcendent dignitas. From this epoch they lost the mandate of Heaven and the Holy Empire was no longer anything but a survival. Frederick III of Austria was the last Emperor crowned in Rome after the rite had reduced itself to an empty and soulless ceremony.

Most great traditional epochs are characterized by a constitution of feudal form, which suits better than any other the regular formation of their structures. Where the accent is placed on the principle of the plurality and of the political autonomy of particular units, there appears at the same time the true place of this universal principle, of this unum quod non est pars, capable of ordering and unifying them really, not by opposing itself to each of them but by dominating them thanks to the transcendent, supra-political and regulative function to which it corresponds, as Dante taught. One finds then a royalty that accords with the feudal aristocracy, an oecumenical imperiality that does not touch the autonomy of the principalities or of the particular kingdoms and that integrates, without denaturing them, the particular nationalities. When, on the contrary, the dignitas declines that allows one to throne above the multiple, the temporal and the contingent, and when, on the other hand, the capacity for a fides, for an allegiance more than simply material, diminishes on the part of each subordinate element, then arises the centralizing tendency, the political absolutism that seeks to maintain the cohesion of the ensemble by means of a violent, political and state unity, and no longer essentially supra-political and spiritual. Or else the processes of pure particularism and of dissociation take the upper hand. By these two ways the destruction of medieval civilization is accomplished. The kings begin to claim for their particular units the principle of absolute authority proper to the Empire, materializing it and finally proclaiming the new and subversive idea of the national State. An analogous process makes arise a multitude of communes, of free cities and of republics, of entities that tend to constitute themselves each for itself, passing to resistance and to revolt not only against the imperial authority but also against the nobility. The summit lowers, the European ecumen undoes itself. The principle of a unique legislation, leaving however a sufficient field to the jus singulare, corresponding to a unique language and to a unique spirit, disappears. Chivalry itself declines and, with it, the ideal of a human type formed by purely ethical and spiritual principles.

The knights come to defend the rights and to support the temporal ambitions of their princes and, finally, of the national States. The great rallies inspired by the supra-political ideal of the holy war and of the just war give place to the combinations, warlike or peaceful, erected in growing number by diplomatic skill. Not only does Christian Europe assist, inert, at the fall of the Eastern Empire and of Constantinople provoked by the Ottomans, but a king of France, Francis I, gives the first blow to the myth of Christendom, base of European unity, by not hesitating, in his struggle against the representative of the Holy Roman Empire, not only to support the Protestant princes in revolt but even to ally himself with the Sultan. The League of Cognac saw the head of the Church of Rome follow the same path. One assisted at this absurdity: Clement VII, ally of the House of France, enters the lists against the emperor by allying himself to the Sultan precisely at the moment when the advance of Suleiman II in Hungary threatened all of Europe and when Protestantism in arms was in the process of overturning its center. One will see likewise a priest in the service of the House of France, Richelieu, support again, in the last phase of the Thirty Years’ War, the Protestant league against the emperor, until, after the Peace of Augsburg, the treaties of Westphalia suppress the last remains of religious element, decree the reciprocal tolerance between the Protestant nations and the Catholic nations and accord to the revolted princes an almost complete independence vis-à-vis the Empire. From this epoch the supreme interest and the stake of the conflicts will no longer be at all the ideal defense of a dynastic or feudal right, but a simple dispute around pieces of European territory: the Empire is definitively supplanted by the imperialisms, that is to say by the maneuvers of the national States desirous of affirming themselves militarily or economically on the other nations. The House of France played, in this upheaval, both on the plane of European politics and in its function distinctly anti-imperial, a preponderant role.

In the ensemble of these developments, and outside the crisis of the imperial idea, the notion itself of sovereignty secularizes itself more and more. The king is no longer anything but a warrior, the political chief of his State. He still incarnates, for a certain time, a virile function and an absolute principle of authority, but which no longer refer to a transcendent reality, except in the residual and empty formula of the divine right such as it was defined, for the Catholic nations, after the Council of Trent, in the period of the Counter-Reformation. The Church declared itself ready to sanction and to consecrate the absolutism of sovereigns intimately deconsecrated, on condition that they make themselves the secular arm of this same Church which followed henceforth the path of indirect action. That is why, in the course of the period consecutive to the decline of the Ghibelline ecumen, there disappears little by little, in each State, the premise by virtue of which the opposition to the Church could be pursued on the base of a superior sense: a more or less exterior recognition is accorded to the authority of Rome in the matter of simple religion, each time that one can obtain, in exchange, something useful to the reason of State. Or else one assists at open attempts to subordinate directly the spiritual to the temporal, as in the Anglican or Gallican movement and, later, in the Protestant world, with the national Churches controlled by the State. Advancing into the modern age, one will see the fatherlands constitute themselves into as many true schisms and oppose themselves to one another, not only as political and temporal units but also as quasi-mystical quantities refusing to admit any superior authority whatever. In any case a point appears clearly: if henceforth the Empire declines and only survives itself, its adversary the Church, although having the field free, does not know how to assume the inheritance, giving thus the decisive proof of its incapacity to organize the West according to its own ideal, that is to say according to the Guelf ideal. What succeeds the Empire is not the Church, is not a strengthened Christendom, but a multiplicity of national States, more and more intolerant with regard to every superior principle of authority.

On the other hand, the deconsecration of the princes, as well as their insubordination vis-à-vis the Empire, by depriving the organisms of which they are the heads of the chrism of a higher principle, push them fatally into the orbit of inferior forces, which will take progressively the upper hand. In general it is fatal that each time a caste revolts against the superior caste and renders itself independent, it loses the specific character that it had in the hierarchical ensemble, to reflect that of the immediately inferior caste. Absolutism, materialist transposition of the traditional unitary idea, prepares the ways of demagogy and of the anti-monarchical national revolutions. And there where the kings, in their struggle against the feudal aristocracy and in their work of political centralization, were carried to favor the claims of the bourgeoisie and of the plebs itself, the process accomplishes itself more rapidly. It is with reason that attention has been drawn to the figure of Philip the Fair, who already announces and contains in germ the different stages of the process of decadence of the modern world. It is Philip the Fair, in effect, who, in destroying, in agreement with the pope, the Templars, destroyed at the same time the most characteristic expression of this tendency to reconstitute the unity of the warrior element and of the sacerdotal element, which was the secret soul of chivalry. It is he who began the work of lay emancipation of the State vis-à-vis the Church, pursued almost without interruption by his successors, just as was pursued, especially by Louis XI and Louis XIV, the struggle against the feudal nobility, struggle that did not disdain the support of the bourgeoisie and tolerated even, to attain its goal, the spirit of revolt of still lower social layers. It is he who already favored an antitraditional culture, thanks to his legists who were, before the humanists of the Renaissance, the true precursors of modern laicism. If it is significant that it is a priest, Cardinal Richelieu, who affirmed, against the nobility, the principle of centralization, in preparing the replacement of the feudal structures by the modern leveling binomial of government and nation, it is incontestable that Louis XIV, in fashioning the public powers, in systematically developing the national unity and in reinforcing it on the political, military and economic plane, prepared, so to speak, a body for the incarnation of a new principle, that of the people, of the nation conceived as simple bourgeois or plebeian collectivity. Thus the anti-aristocratic work undertaken by the kings of France, whose constant opposition against the Holy Empire has already been underlined, was to turn logically, with a Mirabeau, against them and chase them finally from the contaminated throne. One can affirm that it is precisely for having engaged itself first in this way and having, by this fact, constantly increased the centralizing and nationalist character of the notion of State, that France knew first the collapse of the monarchical regime and, in a precise and open fashion, with the advent of the republican regime, the passage of power into the hands of the Third Estate. She became thus, within the European nations, the principal foyer of this revolutionary ferment and of this lay and rationalist mentality, which were to destroy the last vestiges of traditionality.

There is another complementary aspect of the historical Nemesis that is no less precise and interesting. To the emancipation, vis-à-vis the Empire, of the States become absolute, was to succeed the emancipation, vis-à-vis the State, of the sovereign, free and autonomous individuals. One usurpation called and prepared the other, until, in the States that, as sovereign national States, had fallen into étatisation and anarchy, the usurped sovereignty of the State bowed before popular sovereignty, in the framework of which authority and law are legitimate only in the measure where they express the will of the citizens considered as particular individuals and sole sovereigns, awaiting the last phase, the purely collectivist phase. Outside the causes from above that determined the fall of medieval civilization, those from below, distinct although solidary with the first, must not be neglected. Every traditional organization is a dynamic formation that supposes forces of chaos, inferior impulses and interests, lower social and ethnic layers, that a principle of form dominates and brakes: it implies the dynamism of two antagonistic poles, of which the superior pole, inherent to the supranatural element of the superior castes, seeks to train the other toward the height, while the other, the inferior pole linked to the mass, the demos, seeks to attract the first toward the low. Thus, to every weakening of the representatives of the superior principle, to every deviation or degeneration of the summit, correspond, in the fashion of a counterpoint, an emergence and a liberation in the sense of a revolt of the lower layers. From the fact of the processes already analyzed, the right to demand from the subjects the fides, in the double sense, spiritual and feudal, of the word, was to decline progressively, however that the same processes opened virtually the way to a materialization of this fides in a political sense, and, then, to the revolt in question. In effect, while the fidelity spiritually founded is unconditional, that which relates itself to the temporal plane is, on the contrary, conditioned and contingent, subject to revocation according to the circumstances and for empirical motives, and the dualism, the persistent opposition of the Church to the Empire, were to contribute, for their part, to bring every fides back to this inferior and precarious level.

Already in the Middle Ages the Church experiences no scruples in blessing the infraction to the fides by ranging itself on the side of the Italian Communes, in supporting morally and materially the revolt which, outside its exterior aspect, expressed simply the insurrection of the particular against the universal, and inspired itself from a type of social organization no longer resting at all on the warrior caste but directly on the third caste, that of the bourgeois and of the merchants. These usurp the dignity of political power and of the right to arms, fortify their cities, raise their standards, organize their militias against the imperial cohorts and the defensive alliance of the feudal nobility. It is here that begins the movement from below, the uprising of the tide of inferior forces. The Communes prefigure the entirely profane and antitraditional ideal of a democratic organization founded on the economic and mercantile factor and on the Judaic traffic of gold, but their revolt demonstrates above all that the sentiment of the spiritual and ethical sense of loyalism and of hierarchy was, already at that time, on the point of extinguishing. One no longer recognizes in the Emperor anything but a political chief, to whose political pretensions one can resist. One affirms this bad liberty that will destroy and misrecognize every principle of true authority, so as to leave the inferior forces to themselves, and to make all political forms descend onto a purely human, economic and collective plane, ending in the omnipotence of the merchants and, later, of the organized workers. It is significant that the principal foyer of this cancer was the Italian soil, cradle of romanitas. In the struggle of the Communes supported by the Church against the imperial armies and the corpus saecularium principum, one finds the last echoes of the struggle between the North and the South, between tradition and anti-tradition. Frederick I, figure that the plebeian falsification of Italian patriotic history has striven to discredit, fought in reality in the name of a superior principle and of a duty that his function itself imposed on him, against a lay and particularist usurpation founded, among other things, on unilateral ruptures of pacts and of oaths. Dante saw in him the good Barbarossa, legitimate representative of the Empire, which is the source of every true authority. He considered the revolt of the Lombard cities as illegal and factious, conforming to his noble contempt for the new people and the sudden gains, elements of the new and impure communal power, just as he had recognized a subversive heresy in the free regime of particular peoples and in the new nationalist idea. In reality it was not so much to impose a material recognition and to satisfy territorial ambitions as in the name of an ideal claim and for the defense of a supra-political right that the Ottos and then the Swabians fought: not as Teutonic princes but as Roman Emperors, romanorum reges, thus supranational, that they demanded obedience. For honor and for the spirit they fought against the race of merchants and of bourgeois in arms, and that is why these were considered as rebels, less against the emperor than against God, obviare Deo. By divine order, jubente Deo, the prince combats them as representative of Charlemagne, with the avenging sword, to restore the ancient order: redditur res publica statut votuta.

If one continues to consider above all Italy, the Signories, counterparts or successions of the Communes, appear as another aspect of the new climate of which The Prince of Machiavelli is a barometric index. One no longer conceives, as chief, only the powerful individual who does not dominate by virtue of a consecration, by virtue of his nobility, because he represents a superior principle and a tradition, but who dominates in the name of himself, makes use of cunning and of violence, appeals to the resources of politics understood henceforth as an art, as a technique devoid of scruples. Honor and truth have for him no sense, and he uses eventually religion itself only as an instrument among others. Dante had said rightly of the princes of the Italians: qui non heroico more sed plebeo, secuntur superbiam. The substance of this government is therefore not heroic but plebeian. It is to this level that the ancient virtus is lowered, as well as the superiority in relation to good and to evil inherent to him who dominated by virtue of a non-human law. One sees reappear here the type of certain tyrants of antiquity, and one finds at the same time the expression of this unleashed individualism that characterizes, under multiple forms, this turning of history. One can finally see in it the brutal prefiguration of absolute politics and of the will to power that will reaffirm itself, on a much vaster scale, at a recent epoch, during the emergence of the Fourth Estate. These processes mark therefore the end of the cycle of the medieval restoration. In a certain way the gynecocratic-meridional idea reaffirms itself, in the frameworks of which the virile principle, outside the extreme forms just mentioned, and even when it is incarnated in the figure of the monarch, has only a material sense, political and temporal, while the Church remains depository of spirituality under the lunar form of devotional religion and, at best, of contemplation, in the monastic Orders. This scission once confirmed, the right of blood and of the earth or the manifestations of a simple will to power impose their supremacy. The particularism of the cities, of the fatherlands and of the nationalisms is the inevitable consequence, as well as, later, the beginning of the revolt of the demos, of the collective element, subsoil of the traditional edifice, which will tend to seize the leveled structures and the unified public powers created in the previous anti-feudal phase. The most characteristic struggle of the Middle Ages, that of the heroic-virile principle against the Church, aborted. Henceforth Western man tends to autonomy and to emancipation from the religious bond only under the form of a contaminating deviation and going, politically, up to what one could call a demonic reversal of Ghibellinism, which the use the Germanic princes made of the ideas of Lutheranism already prefigured. In a general manner, as civilization, the West emancipates itself from the Church and from the Catholic vision of the world, after the Middle Ages, only by secularizing itself and by sinking into naturalism, by exalting as a conquest the impoverishment proper to a point of view and a will that no longer recognize anything beyond man nor beyond what is entirely conditioned by the human.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is exact. To recognize the decline of the medieval ecumen and the birth of the nations is to hold the distinction between the sacral imperial form that ordered a multiplicity of particular units under a higher measure and the processes that lowered the summit, materialized sovereignty, and released the forces from below. The first measured authority by the capacity to stand above the contingent and to integrate without denaturing. The second measured authority by political absolutism, by national particularism, and by the progressive ascent of the third caste and of the demos. Without that distinction the passage from the Ghibelline Middle Ages to the modern world appears only as a political evolution, and the precise character of the loss of the transcendent dignitas and of the fides remains invisible. The subsequent chapters will examine unrealism and individualism, and the regression of the castes.
