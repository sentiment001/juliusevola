Games and Victory

“In classical antiquity the games (ludi) had a sacred character and therefore became typical expressions of the traditional path of action. ‘Ludorum primum initium procurandis religionibus datum,’ wrote Livy. It was considered dangerous to neglect the sacred games.” Revolt Against the Modern World

In the traditional world the games were res divinae. Livy records that their first institution was given for the procurement of religion. It was considered dangerous to neglect the sacred contests. When the state’s funds were depleted the games could be simplified, never suppressed. An ancient Roman law required the duumvirs and the aediles to have the games celebrated in honor of the gods. Vitruvius held that every city should possess its own theater for the spectacles of the games on the festive days of the immortal gods. Originally the person who presided over the games in the Circus Maximus was also the priest of Ceres, Liber, and Libera. In Rome the games were always presided over by a representative of the official patrician religion, and for certain games special priestly colleges were formed. The ludi were so closely bound to the temples that Christian emperors were obliged to keep the temples open, since their suppression would have entailed the suppression of the games. These games outlasted most of the institutions of ancient Rome and ended only with the Empire itself. An agape to which the demons were invited closed them, signifying a ritual participation in the mystical force associated with them. Augustine reported that the scenic games were written among the divine things by the most learned men.

What today corresponds to sport and the plebeian passion for it was therefore res divinae. In the Hellenic tradition the institution of the most important games stood in close relation to the idea of the struggle of Olympian, heroic, and solar forces against natural and elemental forces. The Pythian games at Delphi recalled Apollo’s triumph over Python and the victory of this Hyperborean god in the contest with the other gods. The Nemean games recalled the victory of Heracles over the Nemean lion. The Olympic games were likewise bound to the idea of the triumph of the celestial race over the titanic race. Heracles, the demigod allied to the Olympians against the giants in the enterprises that mainly relate to his passage to immortality, was said to have created the Olympic games, taking symbolically from the land of the Hyperboreans the olive that would crown the victors. These were virile events, exclusive to men. The action of the games was inscribed in a framework of material symbols of elevated significance, capable of conferring greater efficacy on the magical method and technique concealed within them. They always began with sacrifices and were often celebrated to invoke the divine forces in moments of national peril. The victory of the individual competitor renewed and reanimated, in the individual and in the collectivity, the victory of the Uranian forces over the infernal ones, transforming itself into a principle of fortune.

The circus itself became, from one point of view, a concilium of divinities. The two horsemen who entered the arena, one by the eastern door and the other by the western, to engage in mortal combat, with the original colors of the two factions that were those of the Orphic cosmic egg, white symbolizing winter and red summer, or more exactly the telluric-lunar power and the Uranian-solar power, evoked the struggle of two great elemental forces. Each finishing post of the arena was considered living, a lithos empsychos. The altar built for the god Consus, a telluric demon awaiting the blood poured out in the bloody games or munera, near the finishing post and uncovered only on the occasion of the games, appeared as the point of irruption of the infernal forces, corresponding to the Etruscan puteal. Above stood the statues of the triumphal divinities that again evoked the opposed Uranian principle. Empty seats ritually attested the invisible presence. The athletic or scenic unfolding of the action thus stood, on another plane, as a magical evocation that carried an effective risk in an order of realities far vaster than the life of those who took part in the certamina. The Apollonian games, for example, were instituted on the occasion of the Punic wars to defend against a peril prophesied by the oracle. They were repeated to avert a plague, after which they became the object of periodic celebrations. The success renewed the life and the presence of the triumphal force, feeding it with new energies awakened and formed according to the same orientation.

On this basis it is understood that the victor arose invested with a divine character, at times even as a temporary incarnation of a divinity. At Olympia, in the moment of triumph, the victor was recognized precisely as an incarnation of the local Zeus, and the acclamation of the victorious gladiator passed into the ancient Christian liturgy itself. The Roman triumph merged the military and the divine significances. In the victor, the chief, the imperator acclaimed on the battlefield, was sensed the momentary manifestation of a divine force that transfigured and transhumanized him. Because victory was seen as a more than merely human event, the victory of a chief often assumed for the Romans the features of a numen, an independent divinity whose mysterious life was made the center of a special system of rituals designed to feed it, enliven it, and confirm its invisible presence among men. The most known example is the Victoria Caesaris. Each victory was believed to actualize a new center of forces, separate from the particular individuality of the mortal man who had realized it; or, by victory the victor had become a force existing in an almost transcendent order: a force not of the victory achieved in a given moment of history, but of a perpetual or perennial victory. The cult of Victory, believed to have prehistoric origins, can be said more generally to be the secret spirit of the greatness of Rome and of Rome’s faith in its prophetic destiny.

The notions of soul, double or demon, then of Fury or Erinys, finally of goddess of death and of goddess of victory, often fused in antiquity into a single notion, to the point of giving rise to the idea of a divinity that is at once a goddess of battles and a transcendental element of the human soul. This holds for the Nordic fylgja and the Iranian fravashi. The fylgja, literally the companion, was conceived as a spiritual entity residing in each man and capable of being seen in certain exceptional circumstances, for example at the moment of death or of a mortal peril. It confounds itself with the hugir, which equates to the soul yet is at the same time a supernatural force, spirit of the individual as well as of his stock. It often corresponds to the Valkyrie as the entity of destiny that leads the individual to victory and to heroic death. The fravashi of the ancient Iranian tradition are terrible goddesses of war who give fortune and victory, while also appearing as the internal power of each being, that which sustains him and causes him to be born and to subsist, and as the permanent and divinized soul of the dead, in relation with the mystical force of the stock. The double or demon transcends each of the personal and particular forms in which it manifests. The abrupt passage from the ordinary state of individuated consciousness to that of the demon as such would generally signify a destructive crisis, the crisis and destruction that are verified with death. In special circumstances the double can, as it were, irrupt into the I and make itself completely felt according to its destructive transcendence. The double or demon of man identifies itself with the divinity of death that manifests, for example as Valkyrie, at the moment of death or of a mortal peril. In the ascesis of the religious and mystical type, mortification, renunciation of the I, and the impulse of submission to God are the preferred means of provoking this crisis and of surpassing it. According to the other way, the means is the active exaltation, the awakening of the element action in the pure state.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. The games were not entertainment. They were res divinae that renewed, in the individual and in the collectivity, the victory of Uranian and solar forces over elemental and infernal ones. The victor stood as the temporary incarnation of a divine force, and each victory actualized a perennial center of forces independent of the mortal man who had realized it. The correspondence between the physical and the metaphysical made the material victory the outward and visible sign of a consecration and a mystical rebirth. To reduce the games to sport and the passion for sport is to leave the path of action as pure dissipation. To recover the condition under which the certamina renew the triumphal force is to recover the knowledge that still belonged to the traditional world concerning action under a higher measure. The recognition that the cult of Victory was the secret spirit of Rome’s greatness is the condition for seeing why that knowledge has been driven out of the modern age and what is required to stand against the leveling that has replaced it.
