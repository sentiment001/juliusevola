The Soul of Chivalry

“Within a nominally Christian world, chivalry upheld without any substantial alterations an Aryan ethics in the following things: (1) upholding the ideal of the hero rather than the saint, and of the conqueror rather than of the martyr; (2) regarding faithfulness and honor, rather than caritas and humbleness, as the highest virtues; (3) regarding cowardice and dishonor, rather than sin, as the worst possible evil; (4) ignoring or hardly putting into practice the evangelical precepts of not opposing evil and not retaliating against offenses, but rather, methodically punishing unfairness and evil; (5) excluding from its ranks those who followed the Christian precept ‘Thou Shalt Not Kill’ to the letter; and (6) refusing to love one’s enemy and instead fighting him and being magnanimous only after defeating him.” Revolt Against the Modern World

Traditional nobility, like regality, was originally marked by a spiritual element. That element could be natural or acquired. The second case opens a gap analogous to the one between initiation and investiture. Investiture corresponds to knightly ordination in the West and to the ritual initiation of the warrior caste elsewhere. Initiation itself is a more direct, individual, and inner realization that corresponds to heroic action in the sacral sense, linked to the doctrines of the holy war and of the mors triumphalis. The present chapter examines the first possibility through the spirit and mystery of medieval knighthood.

A clear distinction existed in the European Middle Ages between the feudal aristocracy and the knightly aristocracy. The feudal form was bound to a land and to faithfulness (fides) toward a particular prince. Knighthood appeared instead as a superterritorial and supernational community. Its members were consecrated to a military priesthood. They no longer had a homeland in the ordinary sense and were bound by faithfulness not to persons but to an ethics whose fundamental values were honor, truth, courage, and loyalty, and to a spiritual authority of universal type that was essentially that of the Empire. Knighthood and the great knightly orders of the Christian ecumene formed an essential part of the Empire. They represented the political and military counterpart of what the clergy and the monastic orders represented in the ecclesiastical order. Knighthood was not necessarily hereditary. It was possible to become a knight by performing feats that demonstrated heroic contempt for attachment to life together with the required faithfulness. In the older versions of knightly ordination a knight was ordained by another knight without the intervention of priests, almost as if a force similar to a fluid in the warrior could create new knights by direct transmission. This practice finds a witness in the Indo-Aryan tradition of warriors ordaining other warriors. Later a special religious rite developed for the purpose.

A deeper aspect appears in the dedication of heroic deeds to a woman. The forms this devotion took in European chivalry were extreme enough that, taken literally, they would appear absurd and aberrant. Unconditional faithfulness to a woman was a recurrent theme. According to the theology of the castles there was little doubt that a knight who died for his woman shared the same promise of blessed immortality achieved by a crusader who died to liberate the Temple. Faithfulness to God and to a woman appear to coincide. In some rituals the neophyte knight’s woman had to undress him and lead him to the water so that he could be purified before ordination. The heroes of daring feats involving a woman, such as Tristan and Lancelot, are simultaneously knights of King Arthur committed to the quest for the Grail and members of the same order of heavenly knights to which the Hyperborean Knight of the Swan belonged. Behind these forms stood esoteric meanings that were not disclosed to the judges of the Inquisition or to ordinary people and were therefore often conveyed in the guise of strange customs and erotic tales. The same applies to the woman celebrated by the Ghibelline Love’s Lieges. The woman to whom a knight swears unconditional faithfulness, whom even a crusader consecrates himself to, who leads to purification, whom the knight considers his reward, and who will make him immortal if he dies for her, is essentially a representation of Holy Wisdom or a perceived embodiment, in different degrees, of the transcendent divine woman. She represents the power of a transfiguring spirituality and of a life unaffected by death. This motif belongs to a complete traditional system. A vast cycle of sagas and myths portrays the woman according to this value: Hebe, perennial youth who becomes the spouse of Heracles in the Olympian domain; Idun, whose name means rejuvenation or renewal, and Gunnlöd, holder of the magic potion, who attempt in vain to attain Freya; Brynhild, whom Odin appoints as the earthly bride of a hero who will dare the flickering flame surrounding her hall; the woman of the Land of the Living and of the Victorious One who attracts the Gaelic hero; the Egyptian women who offer the key of life and the lotus of resurrection; the Aztec Teoya-miqui who leads the fallen warriors to the House of the Sun; the well-shaped, strong, and tall-formed maidens who make the soul of the righteous go above the bridge and place it in the presence of the heavenly gods; Ardvi Sura Anahita, strong and holy, who proceeds from the god of light, of whom one asks for the glory that belongs to the Aryan race and to the holy Zarathustra, as well as wisdom and victory; the bride of Gesar, the Tibetan hero, an emanation of the conquering Dolma, related to the double meaning of the Sanskrit śakti as both bride and power; the fravashi, divine women who, like the Valkyries, are simultaneously transcendental parts of the human soul and beings who bestow victory on those who invoke them, favors on those who love them, health on those who are ill. In the Indo-Aryan tradition it is said that not for love of kshatrahood in a material sense is kshatrahood dear, but for love of the soul, the principle of the Self which is light and immortality, kshatrahood is dear. Kshatrahood has deserted him who knows kshatrahood in anything else but the Soul. The same idea may stand behind the aspect of chivalry under consideration.

In some cases the symbolism of the woman may assume a negative, gynaecocratic character different from the character related to the core of chivalry that leads to the ideal of spiritual virility. The persistent, repeated use of feminine characters typical of cycles of a heroic type means nothing else but this: even when confronting the power that may enlighten him and lead him to something more than human, the only ideal of the hero and of the knight is that active and affirmative attitude that in every normal civilization characterizes a true man as opposed to a woman. This is the mystery that in a more or less hidden form shaped a part of the chivalrous medieval literature and was familiar to the Courts of Love, since it was able to confer a deeper meaning to the question whether a woman ought to prefer a cleric or a knight. The Grail tradition had little to do with that of the dominant religion. Although it occasionally adopted some elements from Christianity, it did so the better to express or, conversely, to hide itself. The Grail is truly a myth of the regal religion that confirms what has been said about the secret soul of chivalry. In the outer domain, relative to a general view of life and of ethics, the overall scope of the formative and correcting action that Christianity underwent because of the world of chivalry must be acknowledged. Christianity could not reconcile itself with the ethos of chivalry and espouse the idea of a holy war other than by betraying the principles of that dualistic and escapist spirituality that characterized it over and against the traditional and classical world.

The principles of honor and of faithfulness continued to exist even when the noble was nothing but a king’s officer. Faithfulness is blind when it does not refer, even in a mediated way, to something beyond the human dimension. The qualities that were preserved in the European nobility through heredity eventually underwent a fatal degeneration when they were no longer renewed in their original spirit. The decline of the regal spirituality was unavoidably followed by the decline of nobility itself and by the advent of the forces found in a lower order. Within a nominally Christian world, chivalry upheld without any substantial alterations an Aryan ethics: the ideal of the hero rather than the saint and of the conqueror rather than the martyr; faithfulness and honor rather than caritas and humbleness as the highest virtues; cowardice and dishonor rather than sin as the worst possible evil; methodical punishment of unfairness and evil rather than the precepts of not opposing evil and not retaliating; exclusion from its ranks of those who followed “Thou shalt not kill” to the letter; refusal to love one’s enemy and the practice of fighting him while remaining magnanimous only after defeating him. In reality, chivalry was animated by the impulse toward a traditional restoration in the highest sense of the word, with the silent or explicit overcoming of the Christian religious spirit.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. Nobility that has lost the spiritual element, whether natural or acquired through the knightly path, has already begun its descent. The woman of the knight is not a private sentiment. She is the power that elevates. The Grail is not a Christian legend. It is a residual expression of the regal religion. Honor and faithfulness remain binding only when they refer beyond the human. When that reference is lost, heredity preserves the forms while the force withdraws, and lower orders advance. The recognition of the difference between the feudal officer and the knight of the Empire is the condition for seeing what chivalry still carried and why its later degeneration was inevitable once the higher measure was abandoned.
