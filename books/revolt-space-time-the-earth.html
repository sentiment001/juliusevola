Space, Time, the Earth

“For traditional man space, time, and causality had a very different character than they have in the experience of modern man. The mistake of epistemology from Kant onward is to assume that these fundamental forms of human experience have always remained the same.” Revolt Against the Modern World

The difference between traditional and modern man is not only a difference of mentality or of civilization type. It reaches the experiential possibilities available to each and the way the world of nature is lived according to the categories of perception and the fundamental relation between I and not-I. Space, time, and causality possessed, for traditional man, a character entirely different from the one that corresponds to the experience of recent epochs. The error of the theory of knowledge from Kant forward consists in supposing that these fundamental forms of human experience have always been identical, and in particular identical with those familiar to contemporary man. A profound transformation has occurred here as well, in conformity with the general process of involution. The present chapter limits itself to the difference concerning space and time, and then to the related meaning of the earth.

Modern man experiences time as the simple irreversible order of consecutive events. Its parts are mutually homogeneous and therefore measurable in a quantitative fashion. A distinction is made between before and later, past and future, in reference to a totally relative point that is the present. The fact that an event occurs at one moment rather than another confers no particular quality on the intimate nature of the event itself. Even recent physical-mathematical views that treat time as a fourth dimension or as relative to the reference system remain within this quantitative and indifferent order. Traditional experience of time was of a different kind. Time was not regarded quantitatively but qualitatively, not as a series but as rhythm. It did not flow uniformly and indefinitely. It broke into cycles and periods in which every moment possessed its own meaning and specific value in relation to all the others, together with a lively individuality and functionality. The Chaldean and Hellenic great year, the Etruscan and Latin saeculum, the Iranian aeon, the Aztec suns, and the Hindu kalpas are examples of such structures. Each cycle is both different in its particular form and identical in its phases. The result is a hierarchy based on analogical correspondences between great and small cycles, a reduction of the temporal manifold to a supertemporal unity. Numbers such as seven, nine, twelve, or a thousand function as symbols of rhythmic structure rather than as fixed quantities. A duration that is chronologically longer or shorter can still be equivalent if it encompasses the same structural moments.

Sacred calendars, preserved by priestly wisdom, measured time by these qualitative units and associated specific moments with celebrations and rites. The liturgical year of Catholicism still echoes the older practice. Stellar positions that express higher meanings through cosmic symbolism (the solstices, the points of the zodiac) were transmitted almost identically across traditional forms. The winter solstice appears as the critical point at which the solar force triumphs over the dark period, often symbolized by the axe that splits the year or by myths of solar heroes overcoming opposing powers. Time also possessed a magical aspect. Certain periods were auspicious or inauspicious for rites and for action. Roman military practice, for example, observed precise moments so that human rhythm would stand in syntony with natural and invisible powers. This was not fatalism. It was the concrete alignment that made the action effective. Myths and legends of invisible figures (Alexander, Arthur, Frederick, Sebastian, or the Hindu avatars) show the same structure repeating: historical events or heroic figures can incarnate a metahistorical theme. The chain of eternities is actualized on a symbolic plane. What modern scholarship treats as myth or antihistory often preserves the qualitative content that linear chronology has lost.

If the experience of time differed, the experience of space differed in a parallel way. Modern space is the simple container of bodies and motions, indifferent to both. Even mathematical conceptions of curved or multi-dimensional space remain tied to matter, energy, and gravitation and lack an extraphysical or qualitative correspondence. In the experience of traditional man, and even in residual forms still found among some populations called primitive, space is alive and saturated with qualities and intensities. The traditional idea of space frequently coincides with the idea of a vital ether (the Hindu ākāśa or the mana of other traditions), a mystical substance-energy that penetrates everything, more psychic than physical, often conceived as light, and distributed according to varying saturations in different regions. Each region therefore possesses its own virtues and participates essentially in the powers that reside in it, so that every place becomes fatidic, endowed with its own intensity and occult individuality. The known Pauline expression that in God we live and move and have our being can be applied, with the substitution of the divine or the numinous for the personal God, to what space often was for traditional man. Directions correspond to specific influences. This correspondence is the basis of augural science, of geomancy (developed characteristically in the Far East), and of the doctrine of sacred orientations in the rite and in the arrangement of temples. The art of orienting cathedrals was still known in Europe into the Middle Ages. Temples and cities were located by rites that obeyed the law of analogies, extending the human and visible into the cosmic and invisible. The symbolism of the cross arises from the intersection of the vertical (heavenly) and horizontal (earthly) axes, not from a naturalistic observation. The space of the traditional rite is not the space of the moderns. It is living, fatidic, magnetic space in which every gesture has its value, every sign drawn and every word spoken receives a sense of inevitability and eternity, and the operation becomes a kind of decree for the invisible.

Traditional myths complete the data of the senses with symbols of the sacred elements that traditional man perceived in nature. Imagination, free from the exclusive domination of physical sensation, translated subtler impressions into plastic forms. These are not subjective poetizing. They are necessary symbolizations of psychic and numinous realities that vary in expression according to the race yet remain objectively unified. The same holds for myths that modern scholarship classifies as naturalistic, theological, or historical. They reveal a superhistorical content. The mismatch between the myth and what later counts as history is often the sign that history, reduced to the quantitative sequence, has lost the qualitative truth. Originally the border between I and not-I was more fluid. Magical procedures allowed an incursion of the I into the not-I for effective action, while corresponding defenses guarded against the reverse incursion that constitutes the peril of the soul (possession or subjection to collective and elemental forces). Higher civilizations maintained ritual systems of spiritual defense. In the modern world both the positive magical possibility and the clear perception of the peril have largely vanished. The rigidification of the border that accompanies materialization eliminates the older technique while leaving the man exposed to disguised forms of the same peril in collective currents and ideologies.

The relation between man and his earth followed the same dual structure. Traditionally there existed an intimate psychic and living bond between blood and soil. One aspect was naturalistic and telluric, often totemic, binding the stock to the land in a subpersonal way and expressing itself in communal or promiscuous forms of possession. The other aspect was supernaturalistic. It imposed a triumphal seal on the soil, purified it of demonic telluric elements, and reduced the land to a substratum for transcendent powers. In Aryan traditions the glory or the fire that accompanied conquest marked the land with a higher quality. Deities such as Mars or Thor combined the functions of war and victory with dominion over the soil and its elemental forces. Private ownership in the higher sense was aristocratic. Among the Romans the patres, in Egypt the warriors and priests, and among the Nahua-Aztecs the hereditary lineages held the land under sacred limits (the herctum or equivalent) protected by the gods. The gens was bound to its land through its numina. Settlement of new land was an act of creation that repeated the passage from chaos to cosmos. The Icelandic landnama and the lighting of the Aryan altar fire are examples. Inheritance was tied to the perpetuation of the rite: the firstborn often functioned as pontifex, keeper of the sacred fire. The feudal Middle Ages preserved a mediated residue of this order through the sacramentum of fides. Exile was a grave punishment precisely because it severed the man from the sacred soil that sustained his form. When the higher bond degenerates, property passes to lower castes or becomes a mere economic fact. Capitalist alienation and collectivist re-appropriation both reestablish the naturalistic and promiscuous dependency that the supernaturalistic seal had overcome. Nationalism and socialism appear in this light as returns to the telluric collective rather than as recoveries of the older aristocratic relation.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. Time is not an indifferent sequence, space is not an empty container, and the earth is not a commodity. Traditional man lived inside qualitative rhythms, fatidic places, and a dual bond of blood and soil that could be purified and sealed by a higher force. Modern man lives inside quantitative time, homogeneous space, and a relation to land that has lost both the naturalistic vitality and the supernaturalistic seal. To recover the condition under which cycles reduce to a supertemporal unity, under which every place can be fatidic, and under which ownership preserves form rather than dissolving it, is to recover the experiential possibilities that the involution has closed. The recognition that the modern experience of space, time, and the earth is itself a symptom of the same process that has leveled the higher structures is the condition for standing against the reduction of all three to the quantitative and the profane.
