Spiritual Virility

“The blood of the heroes is closer to God than the ink of the philosophers and the prayers of the faithful.” — Julius Evola, Revolt Against the Modern World

Spiritual virility is the solar principle made effective in a man. It is the capacity to stand as a center, not as a dependent, in relation to the higher nature. In the traditional world this quality was the real foundation of both legitimate royalty and the higher castes. The solar man does not approach the transcendent by petition, belief, or emotional surrender. He acts from a principle that is already oriented upward and that possesses, in itself, a non-human stability. The lunar man, by contrast, stands in a feminine relation to the sacred: he receives, he submits, he waits for confirmation from outside. The distinction is not primarily physical. It is ontological. One type carries an active, centralizing force; the other remains peripheral and receptive. Traditional kingship and the true warrior aristocracy rested on the first type. When that force is absent, titles and offices become empty forms.

The solar orientation appears wherever a tradition preserved the memory of a higher origin. The sacred king is not merely a political head; he is the visible pole of an invisible order. His function is to maintain the connection between the lower world of becoming and the higher world of being. He does this by presence and by rite, not by negotiation with the lower forces. The same principle marks the warrior who has undergone the second birth. Initiation does not add a moral improvement to an ordinary man. It awakens a different nature. The man who has passed through it is no longer measured by the standards of the mass. He can face the invisible without dissolving into it, because something in him already belongs to the higher plane. The blood of such men was understood to carry a quality that ordinary blood does not. That is why the saying places the blood of the heroes closer to the divine than either speculative writing or ordinary prayer. The hero does not explain the higher; he stands in it.

The lunar degeneration of the sacred appears when the active center is lost. What remains is a priestly or devotional attitude that treats the higher as an external power to be appeased or invoked from below. The man who has lost spiritual virility experiences the sacred as something that acts upon him rather than as something he can meet on equal terms of being. He studies it, he feels it, he obeys it, but he does not embody the principle that would allow him to command or to transmit it. In the later phases of a tradition this lunar attitude can still preserve forms, yet the forms no longer carry the original force. The rite becomes a repetition performed by men who lack the quality the rite once presupposed. The result is a religion of dependence rather than a path of realization. The traditional world distinguished the two attitudes sharply: one is the way of those who are, the other is the way of those who believe or who seek.

The same distinction governs the relation between action and contemplation. Contemplation, in the higher sense, is not passive absorption. It is the stabilization of the mind on the non-human principle so that the lower nature is ordered by it. Action, in the higher sense, is not agitation. It is the expression of a center that has already been established. Both presuppose the solar quality. When that quality is absent, contemplation collapses into mystical emotionalism and action collapses into mere force or ambition. The traditional warrior and the traditional ascetic were not opposites; they were two expressions of the same virile orientation. Both refused to identify with the flux of becoming. Both measured themselves by a higher measure. The modern reduction of virility to physical strength, sexual potency, or aggressive self-assertion is the final result of the loss of this orientation. What remains is a biological or psychological residue that no longer refers to anything above itself.

The chapter places a direct demand on the reader. Spiritual virility is not a mood or a style. It is the concrete capacity to remain a center when everything in the age pulls toward dispersion, dependence, and horizontal identification. The test is simple: does a man approach the higher by standing in it, or does he approach it by seeking confirmation, permission, or emotional relief from it? The first stance continues the solar tradition. The second continues the lunar degeneration that has already overtaken most of what still calls itself spiritual or religious. Without the recovery of the first stance, every claim to rank, authority, or higher purpose remains a form without substance. The blood of the heroes is closer to God because it already participates in the principle the philosophers only discuss and the faithful only petition.
