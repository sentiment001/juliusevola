Professional Associations and the Arts; Slavery

“When viewed as a relationship between potentiality and act, hierarchy allowed the same motif established at the top to be reproduced in the activities of the different castes or social organisms; though on the plane of different (more or less spiritual) paths of fulfillment, each one retained in its own way the same upward orientation. This is why in the more complete traditional forms, the “sacred” was a light that shone not only on what today are the profane sciences, arts, and professions, but on trades and various material activities as well.” Revolt Against the Modern World

Hierarchy viewed as the relation of potentiality to act allows the same upward motif fixed at the summit to reappear in the activities of every caste. In the more complete traditional forms the sacred is a light that falls not only on what modern language calls the sciences, the arts and the professions, but on trades and material activities as well. Analogical correspondences between planes make the lower sciences, activities and skills capable of serving as symbols of a higher nature. They communicate the meaning already latent in them. Every modern profane science finds its counterpart in a sacred science of organic and qualitative character. That science treats nature as a hierarchy of degrees of reality and of forms of experience, of which the form given to the physical senses is only one. Alchemy is not a primitive chemistry. Astrology is not the superstitious deification of the stars. Both are knowledge organized so as to constitute a science of purely spiritual and metaphysical realities expressed in symbolic form. The same holds for traditional physiology, still preserved in Chinese acupuncture, Japanese ju-jitsu and aspects of Hindu hatha-yoga. Physical activities themselves become capable of ritual transformation into symbolic actions endowed with a transcendent meaning.

In the caste system every profession or trade corresponds to a vocation. The English word “calling” still preserves the double sense. Every product contains a crystallized tradition that a free and personal activity of incomparable skill can activate. Dispositions developed in the exercise of a trade and acknowledged by the social organism are transmitted through the blood as congenital attitudes. Something further is present: the transmission, if not of a full initiation, then of an inner tradition of the art preserved as a sacred and secret thing, an arcanum magisterium. Certain cognitions were credited with a nonhuman origin. The traditions of gods, demons and heroes who first initiated men into the arts express this idea. Janus, god of initiation, was the god of the Collegia Fabrorum in Rome. Congregations of blacksmiths who came from the East brought a new civilization. The oldest temples of Hera, Cupra, Aphrodite-Venus, Heracles-Hercules and Aeneas often stand where copper and bronze were worked. Orphic and Dionysiac mysteries associate themselves with the art of weaving and spinning. Mastery of a given art is the counterpart and the sign of a parallel inner realization. Even where the caste system lacked the rigor of Aryan India, corporations and artisan guilds arose spontaneously for inferior activities. In Rome they date to prehistoric times and reproduce on their own plane the makeup of the patrician gens. The art and the common activity provide the bond and the order that in higher castes were provided by the aristocratic tradition of blood and ritual. The collegium retains a religious character and a virile, semi-military constitution.

When the inner strength of a fides is no longer present, every activity is defined according to its purely material aspect. Equally worthy paths are replaced by an effect-driven differentiation dictated by the type of activity. This is the sense of intermediary forms of social organization such as ancient slavery. As paradoxical as it appears, it was work that characterized the condition of a slave, and not the reverse. When activity in the lower strata was no longer supported by a spiritual meaning, and when instead of an action there was only work, the material criterion took over. Activities related to matter and to the material needs of life appeared degrading and unworthy of any free human being. Work (ponos) came to be seen as something only a slave would engage in. It became almost a sentence. The only dharma possible for a slave was work. The ancient world did not despise labor because it practiced slavery. Because it despised labor it despised the slave. Those who worked could not be anything but slaves. The traditional world willed slavery into being and differentiated, instituted and regulated as a separate social class the mass of those whose way of being could be expressed only through work. Labor as ponos, obscure effort strictly dictated by need, is the opposite of action. The former is the material, heavy, dark pole. The latter is the free pole of human possibilities detached from need. Free men and slaves are the social crystallization of the two ways of performing an action: according to matter, or ritually. Speculative action, asceticism, contemplation, sometimes even play and war, characterize the pole of action against the servile pole of work.

Esoterically the limitations slavery places on one born into that condition correspond to the nature of his given destiny. On the plane of mythological transposition the Jewish tradition is not far from this view when it treats work as the consequence of Adam’s fall and at the same time as an expiation of that transcendental fault. In antiquity the vanquished were often assigned the functions of slaves. This is not barbarian-style materialism. Nothing happens on earth that is not the symbol and the parallel effect of spiritual events. Winning or losing were never mere coincidences. Among primitive populations the person afflicted by misfortune is always regarded as guilty. The outcomes of every struggle are mystical signs or the results of a divine judgment capable of revealing a human destiny. A transcendental convergence appears between the traditional view of the vanquished and the Jewish view of the sinner. Both inherit a fate befitting the dharma of the slave, namely work. Aristotle based slavery on the presupposition that there are men fit only for physical labor who must be dominated and directed by others. A distinction was drawn between barbarians and Hellenes. The Hindu śūdras originally corresponded to the black aboriginal race, the enemy race dominated by the Aryans, which had no other choice but to serve those who were twice-born. The modern world has stigmatized the ancient civilizations that practiced slavery and boasts of having championed the principle of human dignity. This is mere rhetoric. Europeans reintroduced and maintained slavery into the nineteenth century in overseas colonies in heinous forms rarely found in the ancient world. If there ever was a civilization of slaves on a grand scale, the one in which we live is it. No traditional civilization ever saw such great masses of people condemned to perform shallow, impersonal, automatic jobs. In the contemporary slave system the counterparts of lords or enlightened rulers are nowhere to be found. This slavery is imposed subtly through the tyranny of the economic factor and through the absurd structures of a more or less collectivized society. Because the modern view of life in its materialism has taken away from the single individual any possibility of bestowing on his destiny a transfiguring element and of seeing in it a sign and a symbol, contemporary slavery is one of the gloomiest and most desperate kinds of all times. It is no surprise that in the masses of modern slaves the obscure forces of world subversion have found an easy and obtuse instrument. Where that subversion has already triumphed, the vast Stalinist work camps employ the subjection of man to the goals of collectivization and of the uprooting of every value of the personality in a methodical and even satanic way.

In addition to the considerations concerning work as art in the world of Tradition, the objects produced possessed an organic, functional and consistent quality. The Beautiful did not appear as something separated from a privileged category of artistic objects. The mere utilitarian and mercantile character of the objects was totally lacking. Every object had its own beauty and a qualitative value as well as its own function as a useful object. With regard to art in the traditional world, what occurred was the prodigy of the unification of the opposites, the utter compliance with a set of established rules in which every personal élan appears to be sacrificed and suffocated, and the authentic arising of spirituality within an authentic personal creation. Every object did not have the imprint of an individual artistic personality, as happens today with so-called artistic objects. While revealing a choral taste that makes of the object one of many infinite expressions, it had the seal of a spiritual genuineness that prevented it from being called a copy. Such products bore witness to one stylistic personality whose creative activity developed through centuries. Even when a name, whether real, fictitious or symbolic, was known, this was considered irrelevant. Anonymity, not of a subpersonal but of a superpersonal character, was therefore upheld. On this soil what was born and proliferated in all the domains of life were artisans’ creations far from both a shallow, plebeian sense of utility and an extrinsic, afunctional, artificial beauty. This scission reflects the overall inorganic character of modern civilization.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. The sacred light that falls on every activity, the inner tradition transmitted through the guild, and the distinction between action and work as ponos are not historical curiosities. They diagnose the present. When fides is absent every activity collapses into pure materiality, and the mass of men whose being expresses itself only through work is organized into a condition of slavery without masters, without rites, and without the possibility of seeing in one’s destiny a sign. The modern economic order does not abolish slavery. It universalizes it under the form of shallow, automatic labor while removing the hierarchical and transfiguring elements that once made even the lower station capable of an upward orientation. To recover the sense of vocation as calling, to treat one’s work as a potential rite rather than as mere ponos, and to refuse the rhetoric that calls this the triumph of human dignity is the condition for remaining standing inside a civilization that has turned economics into its daemon and its destiny. Without that recovery the individual is left as one more instrument of the forces of dissolution that find their easiest material in the modern slave.
