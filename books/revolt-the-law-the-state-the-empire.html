The Law, the State, the Empire

“As soon as the sacred character of a law is acknowledged and its origins in a non-human tradition, then its authority becomes absolute; this law becomes too sacred to mention, rigid, steadfast and beyond all criticism. Thus every violation of this law is not regarded as a crime against society, but more as a sacrilege or an act of impiety.” — Julius Evola, Revolt Against the Modern World

Traditional law is not a human product. Its authority rests on a non-human origin. In the Vedic formulation ṛta names the order that is simultaneously truth, reality, and law; anṛta is its opposite: falsehood and disorder. Because the law is acknowledged as descending from a higher plane, its force is absolute. Transgression is treated first as sacrilege or impiety, an act that endangers the spiritual condition of the offender and of those bound to him, not merely as a social inconvenience. The modern view reverses the sequence. It begins from empirical usefulness, from the interests of the collective, or from the will of the majority, and then asks whether a rule is convenient. That starting point already closes the law inside the domain of becoming.

The traditional state is the concrete organization of this higher measure. It is not an administrative machine that exists to serve the people. The people exist inside the state, and the state exists as the visible form through which the higher principle orders the lower. Its meaning and purpose are transcendent; they are not inferior to the claim the Church later made for itself as a path to the world above. When the state is reduced to the management of material interests or to the expression of the demos, the hierarchy is inverted. Authority then arises from below and remains closed in the lower nature. Traditional texts treat this inversion as a regression, not as progress.

The empire is the highest political form of the same principle. It is not a large nation or a confederation of interests. It is the earthly symbol of the higher order, the structure that can give concrete expression to a law and a state that remain oriented upward. Its measure is qualitative and universal rather than particular and quantitative. When the imperial function is lost, what remains are particular powers that can organize force or administration but cannot supply a true center. The modern sequence of national states and further fragmentation is the visible consequence of that loss.

Modern conceptions of law and the state locate both in human will, contract, or technical necessity. Each of these is a lower principle. None of them can generate the absolute character that traditional law possessed, nor the transcendent purpose that the traditional state claimed. The result is continuous regulation without form and administration without orientation. The traditional triad of law derived from above, state as its organic embodiment, and empire as its highest political expression existed only as the concrete ordering of collective life according to the distinction between the two natures. Without that distinction the structures become unintelligible.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is the recognition that law, the state, and the empire are either oriented upward or they are not what they claim to be. The modern alternatives of usefulness, consent, force, or technical competence remain closed in the domain of becoming. They can produce regulation and administration. They cannot produce order. To hold this idea is to measure every claim to law and every claim to political form by whether it carries the higher quality or remains a product of the lower. The absence of that quality is the decisive fact of the age.
