The Doctrine of the Castes

“The caste system is one of the main expressions of the traditional sociopolitical order, a ‘form’ victorious over chaos and the embodiment of the metaphysical ideas of stability and justice. The division of individuals into castes or into equivalent groups according to their nature and to the different rank of activities they exercise with regard to pure spirituality is found with the same traits in all higher forms of traditional civilizations, and it constitutes the essence of the primordial legislation and of the social order according to ‘justice.’ Conformity to one’s caste was considered by traditional humanity as the first and main duty of an individual.” Revolt Against the Modern World

The caste system is a form victorious over chaos. It embodies the metaphysical ideas of stability and justice. The division of individuals into castes, or into equivalent groups ordered according to their nature and to the rank of the activities they exercise with regard to pure spirituality, appears with the same traits in every higher form of traditional civilization. It constitutes the essence of the primordial legislation and of the social order according to justice. Conformity to one’s caste was regarded by traditional humanity as the first and main duty of an individual. The most complete type is the ancient Indo-Aryan hierarchy, visibly inspired by the hierarchy of functions in a physical organism animated by the spirit. At the lower level stand the undifferentiated and impersonal energies of matter and of mere vitality. These are regulated by the functions of metabolism and of the organism. Those functions are in turn regulated by the will, which moves and directs the body as an organic whole in space and time. The soul is the center, the sovereign power and the light of the entire organism. The same order holds for the castes: the activities of the workers or slaves (śūdras) are subordinated to those of the bourgeoisie (vaiśya); above them stands the warrior nobility (kṣatriya); and at the summit stand the representatives of spiritual authority and power (the brāhmaṇa in the original sense, and the leaders as pontifices). An analogous pattern appears in the Persian system of the four pishtra, in the medieval European division into servants, burghers, nobility and clergy, and in the Platonic correspondence of rulers, warriors and workers to the powers of the soul and to the regions of the body. The external order reflects an inner order according to justice.

The castes defined functions and typical ways of being and acting more than they defined social groups. The correspondence of the fundamental natural possibilities of the single individual to any of these functions determined his belonging to the corresponding caste. In the duties toward his caste the individual recognized the normal explication, the development and the chrism of his own nature within the overall order imposed from above. For this reason the caste system developed and was applied in the traditional world as a natural, agreeable institution based on something that everybody regarded as obvious. It did not rest on violence, oppression or on what modern language calls social injustice. By acknowledging his own nature, traditional man knew his own place, his function, and the correct relationship with both superiors and inferiors. Failure to acknowledge the authority proper to a higher function was regarded as the result of ignorance rather than as a fault. A hierarchy was not a device of the human will. It was a law of nature, as impersonal as the physical law according to which a lighter fluid floats on top of a denser fluid unless an upsetting factor intervenes. There was a firmly upheld principle: those who want to institute a process at variance with human nature cannot make it function as an ethical system.

What most upsets modern sensitivity is the law of heredity and preclusion. It appears unfair that birth should seal social status and predetermine the type of activity to which a man will consecrate the rest of his life, which he will not be able to abandon without becoming an outcast. Against the background of the traditional view of life these difficulties dissolve. The closed caste system rested on two fundamental principles. The first is that traditional man regarded everything visible and worldly as the mere effects of causes of a higher order. To be born in one condition rather than another, as a man or a woman, in one caste rather than in another, in one race instead of another, and to be endowed with specific talents and dispositions, was not regarded as pure chance. These circumstances corresponded to the nature of the principle embodied in an empirical self, whether willed or already present transcendentally in the act of undertaking human birth. This is one aspect of the Hindu doctrine of karma. Similar teachings appear in the Hellenistic view that the soul’s quality exists before any bodily life and that the body is organized and determined by the image of the soul which is in it, and in certain Persian-Aryan views connecting sacred regality to the path of solar influences. Excluding the idea that birth is a casual event, the doctrine of the castes appears under a different light. Birth does not determine nature. Nature determines birth. A person is endowed with a certain spirit by virtue of being born in a given caste, and at the same time he is born in a specific caste because he possesses, transcendentally, a given spirit. The differences between the castes are the reflection and the confirmation of a preexisting, deeper, and more intimate inequality. They represent a higher application of the principle suum cuique.

In the context of a living tradition the castes represented the natural place of the earthly convergence of analogous wills and vocations. The regular and closed hereditary transmission forged a homogeneous group sharing favorable organic, vitalistic and even psychic proclivities for the regular development of those prenatal determinations. The individual did not receive his nature from the caste. The caste afforded him the opportunity to recognize or remember his own nature and prenatal will, while presenting him with a kind of occult heritage related to that nature. The second principle is thoroughly spiritual. No object or function is in itself superior or inferior to another. The true difference is given by the way in which the object or the function is lived out. The earthly way, inspired by utilitarianism or by greed (sakāma-karma), is contrasted with the heavenly way of the one who acts without concern for the consequences and for the sake of the action itself (niṣkāma-karma), and who transforms every action into a rite and into an offering. Such was the path of bhakti, corresponding more to the virile sense of medieval fides than to the pietistic sense of devotion. An action performed according to this type of bhakti was compared to a fire that generates light and in which the matter of the act itself is consumed and purified. The degree to which the act was freed from matter, detached from greed and passion, and made self-sufficient defined the hierarchy of activities and consequently the hierarchy of the castes. By remaining in his own caste, in faithfulness to his own nature and to the morality of his own caste, everyone enjoyed the same dignity and the same purity as everybody else. This held for a śūdra as well as for a king. Everybody performed his function within the overall social order and, through his own peculiar bhakti, partook of the supernatural principle of that order. The notion of dharma, one’s peculiar nature to which one is supposed to be faithful, comes from the root meaning “to sustain” or “to uphold.” It expresses the element of order, form or cosmos that Tradition embodies and implements over and against chaos and becoming. Through dharma the traditional world is upheld. Leaving one’s caste and mixing castes, or even the rights, the duties, the morality and the cults of each caste, was considered a sacrilege that destroys the efficacy of every rite and leads those who are guilty of it to the realm of demonic influences belonging to the inferior nature. The people guilty of crossing the caste line were the only impure beings in the entire hierarchy. They were pariahs or untouchables because they represented centers of psychic infection in the sense of an inner dissolution. When gold and lead are mixed together, both are altered and both lose their own nature. Mixing subverted the traditional order and opened the door to infernal forces by removing what Goethe called the creative limitation. The goal was the transfiguration of the form, obtained through bhakti and niṣkāma-karma. The alteration or destruction of the form was regarded as a degrading form of escapism. The outcast was the vanquished.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. The caste system is the form that holds chaos in check. The hierarchy of functions is the external expression of an inner hierarchy according to justice. Nature determines birth, and the closed transmission of the caste affords the individual the place in which he can recognize and actualize the spirit he already possesses. Faithfulness to one’s own dharma, performed as rite and as offering, confers the same dignity on every station and upholds the order itself. The modern rejection of this order, the demand for equal rights and equal duties without regard to nature, and the aspiration to move horizontally from one function to another, do not liberate. They dissolve the form, remove the creative limitation, and generate the chaos of existential and psychic possibilities in which most people now live in a state of disharmony and social tension. To know one’s own nature, to will it, and to actualize it ritually through faithfulness is the condition for remaining standing inside an order that still has a center. Without that recognition the hierarchy collapses into the leveling of the present age, and the individual is left with the freedom of a restless puppet whose chain is no longer short.
