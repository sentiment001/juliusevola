The Beginning

“In order to understand both the spirit of Tradition and its antithesis, modern civilization, it is necessary to begin with the fundamental doctrine of the two natures. According to this doctrine there is a physical order of things and a metaphysical one; there is a mortal nature and an immortal one; there is the superior realm of ‘being’ and the inferior realm of ‘becoming.’ Generally speaking, there is a visible and tangible dimension and, prior to and beyond it, an invisible and intangible dimension that is the support, the source, and the true life of the former.” — Julius Evola, Revolt Against the Modern World

The starting point is the doctrine of the two natures. There is a physical order and a metaphysical order. There is a mortal nature and an immortal nature. There is the higher realm of being and the lower realm of becoming. There is what is visible and tangible, and prior to it and beyond it an invisible and intangible dimension that is the support, the source, and the true life of the former. Anywhere in the traditional world, East or West, this was present as an unshakable axis. It was knowledge, not theory. The man of Tradition was aware of a dimension of being wider than what modern men experience and call reality. Today reality is taken as the world of physical bodies located in space and time. Those who admit something beyond the senses are usually led to it by a scientific hypothesis, a speculative idea, or a religious dogma. Their practical and immediate experience remains limited to the physical. This is the real materialism of the age: the fact that ordinary experience no longer reaches non-physical realities. Scientific or philosophical materialisms are secondary. The worst form is not an opinion; it is the restriction of experience itself. Most intellectual revolts against materialist views only strike the outer effects of a deeper restriction that began earlier and in a different historical context.

Traditional experience went beyond those limits. Among some peoples still called primitive a faint echo of older powers remains. In traditional societies the invisible was as real as, or more real than, the data of the senses. It shaped every aspect of individual and social life. From the traditional perspective, what is now called reality was only one species of a much wider genus. The invisible was not automatically equated with the supernatural. The traditional notion of nature did not mean merely the world of bodies and visible forms that modern secular science studies. It corresponded essentially to part of an invisible reality. The ancients had the sense of a dark netherworld populated by obscure and ambiguous forces of every kind, the demonic soul of nature, the essential substratum of all nature’s forms and energies. This stood opposite the superrational and sidereal brightness of a higher region. Nature also included everything that is merely human, because what is human cannot escape birth and death, impermanence, dependence, and transformation. These belong to the inferior region. By definition, “that which is” has nothing to do with human and temporal affairs. The saying holds: the race of men is one thing, and the race of the gods is quite another. This remains valid even though traditional men once held that reference to the superior domain could lead the integration and purification of the human element toward the non-human dimension. Only the non-human dimension was the essence and the goal of any truly traditional civilization.

The world of being and the world of becoming affect things, demons, and men. Every hypostatic representation of these two regions, whether expressed in astral, mythological, theological, or religious terms, reminded the traditional man of the existence of the two states. It also stood as a symbol to be resolved into an inner experience, or at least into the foreboding of one. In the Hindu tradition, and especially in Buddhism, the idea of samsara, the current that dominates and carries away every form of the inferior world, refers to life as blind yearning and as an irrational identification with impermanent aggregates. Hellenism saw nature as the embodiment of the eternal state of deprivation of those realities that, having their own principle and cause outside themselves, flow and run away indefinitely. In their becoming they reveal a primordial and radical lack of direction and purpose and a perennial limitation. Matter and becoming express what acts in a being as an obscure necessity, or as an irrepressible indetermination, or as the inability to acquire a perfect form and to possess itself in a law. What the Greeks called ananke and apeiron, the Orientals called adharma. Christian Scholastic theology shared the same ground when it located the root of every unredeemed nature in cupiditas and appetitus. In different ways the man of Tradition found, in the experience of covetous identification that obscures and impairs being, the secret cause of his existential predicament. The incessant becoming and the perennial instability and contingency of the inferior region appeared as the cosmic and symbolical materialization of that predicament.

The path to the other region, the world of being, what is no longer physical but metaphysical, was the experience of asceticism. Asceticism here consists in mastery over oneself, self-discipline, autonomy, and the leading of a unified life. A unified life is an existence that does not need to be spent in search of other things or other people in order to be complete and justified. The traditional representations of this other region were solar symbols, heavenly regions, beings made of light or fire, islands, and mountain peaks. These were the two natures. Tradition conceived the possibility of being born into either one, and also of going from one birth to another, according to the saying: a man is a mortal god, and a god is an immortal man. The traditional world knew these two great poles of existence and the paths leading from one to the other. It knew the physical world in the totality of its forms, visible or underground, human or subhuman and demonic, and it knew hyperkosmia, a world beyond this world. According to Tradition the former is the fall of the latter, and the latter represents the liberation of the former. Spirituality was held to be something beyond life and death. Mere physical existence, or living, is meaningless unless it approximates the higher world or that which is more than life, and unless one’s highest ambition consists in participating in hyperkosmia and obtaining an active and final liberation from the bond represented by the human condition.

Every authority is fraudulent, every law is unjust and barbarous, every institution is vain and ephemeral unless they are ordained to the superior principle of Being, derived from above, and oriented upward. The traditional world knew divine kingship. It knew the bridge between the two worlds, which is initiation. It knew the two great ways of approach to the transcendent: heroic action and contemplation. It knew the mediation of rites and faithfulness. It knew the social foundation of traditional law and the caste system. It knew the political earthly symbol of the empire. These are the foundations of the traditional hierarchy and civilization. The victorious anthropocentric civilization of the present age has wiped them out.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore the recognition that the restriction of experience to the physical is the decisive fact of the age, not a secondary opinion. The two natures are not a theory to be debated; they are the axis. The lower is the domain of becoming, craving, and dependence. The higher is the domain of being. Everything that claims form, authority, or law is measured by whether it is oriented upward or remains closed in the lower. The traditional structures (divine kingship, initiation, heroic action and contemplation, rites, law and caste, the empire) existed only as the concrete ordering of life according to that distinction. Without the distinction itself, those structures are unintelligible and the revolt against the modern world has no ground.
