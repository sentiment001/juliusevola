Unrealism and Individualism

“The first form under which humanism appears is individualism. It is characterized by the constitution of an illusory centre outside the real centre; by the prevaricating pretence of a ‘self’ that is simply the mortal ‘self’ of the body; and by the construction, through purely natural faculties, of appearances that have no consistency outside that false and vain centre.” Revolt Against the Modern World

To follow the later phases of the decadence of the West one must return to what was said of the first traditional crises, taking as reference the fundamental truth of the world of Tradition relative to the two “regions,” the duality that exists between the world and the supra-world. For traditional man these two regions existed; they were realities. The establishment of an objective and efficacious contact between the one and the other was the preliminary condition of every superior form of civilization and of life. The interruption of this contact, the concentration of all possibilities in a single world, the human and temporal world, the substitution for the experience of the supra-world of ephemeral phantasms evoked by the turbid exhalations of mortal nature: such is the general sense of “modern” civilization, which now enters the phase in which the diverse forces of decadence, which had manifested themselves in earlier epochs but had then been braked by reactions or by the power of opposed principles, attain their full and formidable efficiency.

In its most general sense humanism appears as the stigma and the watchword of the whole new civilization that frees itself from the “darkness of the Middle Ages.” This civilization will know only man: it is in man that all things will begin and end; it is in man that will be found the only heavens and the only hells, the only glorifications and the only maledictions that will henceforth be known. It is this World — the other of the true world — with its creations of fever and of thirst, with its artistic vanities and its “geniuses,” with its jungle of machines and factories, that will constitute for man the limit. The first form under which humanism appears is individualism. It is characterized by the constitution of an illusory centre outside the real centre, by the prevaricating pretence of a “Moi” that is simply the mortal “moi” of the body, and by the construction, through purely natural faculties, of diverse appearances that, outside this false and vain centre, have no consistency: truths and laws marked by the contingency and the caducity inherent in everything that belongs to the world of becoming. From this follows the radical unrealism, the fundamental inorganicity of everything that is modern. Interiorly as well as exteriorly nothing is any longer alive; everything will be construction: being is extinguished, substituted in all domains by “wanting” and by the “I” as a sinister, rationalist and mechanistic propping of a corpse.

On the religious plane unrealism stands in close relation with the loss of tradition. Already the ancient world had witnessed the involutive passage from symbolism to mythology, to a mythology that became continually more opaque and mute and of which the arbitrary of artistic fantasy took possession. When later the experience of the sacred reduced itself to faith, to sentiment and to moralism, and the intuitio intellectualis to a simple concept of scholastic philosophy, the unrealism of the spirit covered almost the totality of the supernatural domain. This tendency continued with Protestantism, of which it is significant that it was contemporary with Humanism and the Renaissance. Abstraction made from its ultimate sense in the history of civilizations, from the antagonist function it exercised in the Middle Ages, and from the absence of an initiatic and esoteric dimension, one must recognize in the Catholic Church a certain traditional character that differentiated it from what simple Christianity had been, thanks to a whole apparatus of dogmas, of symbols, of myths, of rites and of sacred institutions in which there were conserved, even if by reflection, themes of a superior knowledge. By affirming rigidly the principle of authority and of dogma, by defending the character of extra-naturality and of supra-rationality of “revelation” in the domain of knowledge, by defending the principle of the transcendence of grace in the domain of action, the Church defended almost in a desperate manner the non-human character of its deposit against all individualist prevarications. This extreme effort of Catholicism was nonetheless to meet a limit. The dike could not hold. To certain forms that justified themselves on the simply religious plane the character of absolute proper to the non-human could not be conserved, there where not only superior knowledge was lacking but where the secularization of the Church, the corruption and unworthiness of a great part of its representatives, and the importance that political and contingent interests progressively took for it became continually more evident. The climate thus became ever more favorable to a reaction.

Luther’s invectives against the “papacy, the devil’s creature in Rome” and against Rome as the “kingdom of Babylon” and as a radically pagan reality totally inimical to the Christian spirit were very similar to those invectives employed by the early Christians and by the Jewish apocalyptic texts against the city of the eagle and of the battle-axe. From the point of view of the metaphysics of history the positive and objective role of Protestantism is to have brought into light the fact that, no truly spiritual principle being any longer “immediately” and “naturally” present in modern man, the latter was to represent this principle as something transcendent. This is why Catholicism had already assumed the myth of original sin. Protestantism exasperates this myth, sustaining the fundamental impotence of man to attain any salvation alone; generalizing, it considers the whole of humanity as a cursed mass, condemned to accomplish evil automatically, and it adds, to the truth obscurely indicated by this myth, the nuance of a veritable Syrian masochism, which expresses itself in images repugnant to every Aryan spirit. In reality, facing the antique ideal of spiritual virility, Luther does not hesitate to call “royal marriage” those in which the soul, conceived as a “prostitute” and as “the most miserable and most sinful creature,” plays the role of the woman (in De libertate christiana) and to compare man to a poor beast of burden on which God or the devil rides at will, without him being able to prevent it (in De servo arbitrio). Yet while the recognition of this existential situation ought to have entailed as consequence the affirmation of the necessity either of the support proper to a ritual and hierarchical system or of the most severe realizing ascesis, Luther denies both the one and the other. The whole system of Luther is visibly conditioned by the personal equation and by the sombre interiority of its founder, who was a failed monk, a man incapable of overcoming a nature dominated by passion, sensuality, anger and pride.

This personal equation is already reflected in the singular doctrine according to which the Ten Commandments would not have been given to man by the divinity to be realized in life, but so that man, recognizing his impotence to follow them, his nullity, the invincibility of concupiscence and of the tendency to sin, would entrust himself to the god conceived as a person, placing desperately all his hope in free grace. This “justification by pure faith,” this condemnation of works, leads Luther to unleash himself against monasticism and the ascetic life, which he calls “vain and lost life,” thus diverting Western man from these last possibilities of reintegration offered by the purely contemplative life that Catholicism had conserved and that culminated in figures such as those of a Saint Bernard of Clairvaux, a Ruysbroeck, a Saint Bonaventure and a Meister Eckhart. In the second place the Reform denies the principle of authority and of hierarchy on the plane of the sacred. The idea that a being, as pontifex, could be infallible in the matter of sacred doctrine and could therefore legitimately claim an authority admitting no discussion is considered aberrant and absurd, Christ having given to no Church, not even to the Protestant Church, the privilege of infallibility. It therefore belongs to each one to judge, thanks to an individual free examination, in the matter of doctrine and of interpretation of the sacred books, independently of any control and of any tradition. It is not only in the domain of knowledge that the distinction between layman and priest is fundamentally abolished: one denies equally the sacerdotal dignity understood not as an empty attribute but as the dignity of him who, differently from other men, is furnished with a supernatural chrism and bears the imprint of an “indelible character” that permits him to activate the rites (these are vestiges of the antique idea of the “Lord of the Rites”). Thus the objective and non-human sense that dogma and symbol, but also the system of rites and of sacraments, could have is found denied and misrecognized.

It was in the historical development of Protestantism, especially in Anglo-Saxon Calvinism and Puritanism, that the religious idea became increasingly dissociated from any transcendent interest and thus susceptible to being used to sanctify any temporal achievement to the point of generating a kind of mysticism of social service, work, “progress,” and even profit. For quite a long time this theory has supplied an ethical and religious justification for the rise to power of the merchant class and of the Third Estate during the cycle of the modern democracies and capitalism. Rationalism was to develop unrealism up to the forms of absolute idealism. The so-called “critical thought that has arrived at the awareness of itself” declares: “All that is real is rational and all that is rational is real,” by which one has the limit-form of unrealism. But practically a far more important part in the construction of the modern world than such philosophical abstractions was had by rationalism associating itself with empiricism and experimentalism in the frameworks of scientism. Again, the birth of modern naturalistic-scientific thought is almost contemporary with the Renaissance and the Reform, because in all this, at bottom, it is a question of solidary expressions of a single upheaval. Individualism necessarily brings with it naturalism. It is science that, by providing today the means of controlling and of using all the forces of nature according to the ideals of an Ahrimanic conquest of the world, has given birth to the most dangerous temptation that has perhaps ever offered itself to man: that of considering as a glory his own renunciation and of confusing power with the phantasm of power.

This process of detachment, of loss of the superior world, of loss of tradition, of aggressive laicism, of triumphant rationalism and naturalism, manifests itself in an identical fashion on the plane of the relations of man with reality and on the plane of society, of the State and of mores. As was already said in treating the problem of the death of civilization, the intimate submission of the humble and of the man deprived of knowledge to traditional principles and institutions justified itself in the measure where it permitted an efficacious hierarchical relation with beings who knew and who “were,” who attested and maintained living the non-human spirituality of which each traditional law is the body and the adaptation. But when this centre of reference no longer exists, or when there subsists of it only the empty symbol, then submission is vain, obedience sterile: there results a petrification, not a ritual participation. Thus, in the modern world, humanized and deprived of the dimension of transcendence, it was fatal that every law inspired by a principle of hierarchy and of stability should disappear, even on the most exterior plane, and that one should arrive at a veritable atomization of the individual, not only in the matter of religion but also in the political domain, at the same time as the misrecognition of every value, of every institution and of every traditional authority. Once the fides is secularized, to the revolt of souls succeeds the revolt of men; to the revolt against spiritual authority succeed the revolt against temporal power and the claim of the “rights of man,” the affirmation of the liberty and of the equality of each, the definitive abolition of the idea of caste and of privilege, the libertarian disintegration.

But a law of action and reaction wants that every individualist usurpation automatically entail a collectivist limitation. The caste-less, the emancipated slave and the glorified pariah — the modern “free man” — finds before him the net of the other caste-less, and finally the brutal power of the collective. It is thus that the collapse continues: from the personal one regresses into the anonymous, into the herd, into pure chaotic and inorganic quantity. And just as scientific construction has sought, by acting from the exterior, to recompose the multiplicity of particular phenomena henceforth deprived of that interior and veritable unity which exists only on the plane of metaphysical knowledge, so the moderns have sought to replace the unity that resulted, in ancient societies, from living traditions and from sacred law, by an exterior, anonymous, mechanical unity, of which all individuals undergo the constraint, without having any longer among themselves any organic relation and without perceiving superior principles or figures thanks to which obedience is also an assent and submission a recognition and an elevation. Essentially founded on the data of material existence and of purely social life, dominated without light by the impersonal and leveling system of the “public powers,” mass-beings thus make their appearance, who make an inversion of the state of individualism. Whether they present themselves under the mask of democracy or of national States, of republics or of dictatorships, these forms do not delay, moreover, in being carried by independent infra-human forces.

The most decisive episode of the unleashing of the European plebs, namely the French Revolution, already makes appear the typical traits of this upheaval. It permitted one to observe how the forces escape the control of those who have apparently raised them. Once unleashed, one would say that this revolution marched all alone, guiding men more than the latter guided it. One by one it overturns its sons. The leaders, rather than true personalities, appear here as incarnations of the revolutionary spirit, carried by the movement as something inert and automatic. They emerge on the wave as long as they follow the current and serve the ends of the revolution; but as soon as they seek to dominate it or to deviate it, they are eliminated. Members of certain organizations promoted this inversion until they transformed the groups of which they were the heads into active instruments of the diffusion of anti-traditional and rationalist thought. One of the most tangible examples is the role Masonry played in the American Revolution as well as in the underground ideological preparation of the French Revolution and in the revolutions that occurred in Spain, Turkey and Italy, among others. This is how the secret front of world subversion and of counter-tradition was formed, not only through general influences but also through specific centres of action. In this one can recognize again the phenomenon of a pathological inversion of polarity: the person, in his superior faculties, becomes the instrument of other forces that replace him and that, often without his suspecting it, use him for spiritual destructions.

On this basis, in modern activism, instead of a way toward the superindividual — as was the case in the ancient, already indicated possibilities of heroic ascesis — one has therefore a way toward the subindividual; one propitiates and provokes destructive irruptions. The new religions of life and of the “élan vital,” as well as “Faustian” activity and myth, make their appearance and become new intellectual “drugs” that ensure that the movement may not be interrupted but spurred on, so that it may acquire a meaning in itself, both for man and for existence in general. Hetaerism and Amazonism today are also present in new forms, such as the disintegration of the family, modern sensuality, and the incessant and turbid quest for women and immediate sexual gratification, as well as in the masculinisation of the woman, her emancipation, and her standing above men who have become enslaved to their senses or turned into beasts of burden. More than to the survivals of the positive religion that has reigned in the West, one must attach today a value of symptom to the obscure evocations proper to the diverse mediumistic-spiritualist and theosophical currents, to the currents oriented toward the valorization of the subconscious, to the mystical currents of pantheistic and naturalistic foundation, whose proliferation presents a particularly epidemic character there where, as in the Anglo-Saxon countries, the materialization of the virile type and of daily existence has reached its maximum and where Protestantism has impoverished and secularized the religious ideal itself. Thus the parallelism is complete and the cycle is in the process of closing.
