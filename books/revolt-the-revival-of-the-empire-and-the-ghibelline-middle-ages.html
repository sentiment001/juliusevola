The Revival of the Empire and the Ghibelline Middle Ages

“The idea of the Empire reaffirms itself once more in the Ghibelline Middle Ages as a supernatural universal institution created by Providence as a remedium contra infirmitatem to rectify the fallen nature and direct men toward eternal salvation.” Revolt Against the Modern World

After the syncope of the Western tradition the imperial idea does not disappear. It is translated. The translatio imperii carries the Roman principle northward. Germanic peoples of pure Nordic origin, driven by the migrations into the space of Roman civilization, take up the formative force of the ancient romanitas. Charlemagne and the Carolingian revival mark one stage. The decisive medieval form is the Holy Roman Empire. Here the Germanic element defends the imperial idea against the Church and awakens the ancient Roman principle to new life.

The conflict between Empire and Papacy is not a mere political rivalry. It is the opposition of two incompatible spiritualities. The Ghibelline ideal asserts the sacral character of temporal authority in continuity with the Roman inheritance. It tends secretly toward the reconstitution of the original unity of the two powers: the royal and the hieratic, the sacred and the virile. The Guelf ideal claims both swords for the priest and reduces the Empire to a beneficium conferred by the Pope. In this view the Emperor owes the Church the same vassalage a feudatory owes his lord. Because the spirituality the head of the Roman Church could embody remained essentially that of the servants of God, this Guelfism does not restore the primordial solar unity of the two powers. It shows how far Rome had moved from its ancient tradition. It represents the opposed principle: the domination of the truth of the South. In the confusion of symbols the Church claims the Sun against the Moon for itself while adopting the symbol of the Mother and treating the Emperor as one of her sons. Guelf supremacy therefore expresses a return to the old gynecocratic vision: the authority of the maternal principle over the masculine principle bound to temporal and perishable reality.

The high point of this revival is the Hohenstaufen dynasty. Around them the idea of a divine and Roman stock affirmed itself. These emperors were not merely holders of the regnum. They were capable of penetrating the mysteries of God that others could only sense through images. Frederick I Barbarossa appears as the warrior-king who reestablishes order through force and charismatic presence. Henry VI consolidates and organizes. Frederick II carries the claim furthest. Popular prophecies of the period already announced that the high cedar of Lebanon would be cut, that there would be only one God (that is, one monarch), and that woe would fall on the clergy; if it fell, a new order was ready. The Dry Tree, the sleeping or never-dead emperor hidden in the mountain or in an inaccessible center, the last battle against the hordes of Gog and Magog—these themes attach to the Ghibelline emperor. He is the force that must awaken at the end of a cycle, make the Dry Tree flower, and fight the final battle.

Chivalry preserves, almost without alteration, a Nordic-Aryan ethic inside a world that was only nominally Christian. It takes the hero rather than the saint, the victor rather than the martyr, as its ideal. It places the sum of values in fidelity and honor rather than in charity and humility. It treats cowardice and shame as a worse evil than sin. It does not respect the rule that one must not resist evil and must return good for evil; it punishes the unjust. It excludes from its ranks anyone who would have taken the Christian precept do not kill literally. Its principle is not to love the enemy but to fight him and to be magnanimous only after victory. The trial by arms, the solution of every problem by force regarded as a virtue entrusted by God to man so that justice, truth, and right may triumph on earth, extends from the domain of honor and feudal law into the theological domain. The ordeal was proposed even in matters of faith. This idea is not Christian. It refers to the mystical doctrine of victory that ignores the dualism proper to religious conceptions, unites spirit and power, and sees in victory a kind of divine consecration. The attenuated theistic interpretation according to which the Middle Ages thought of a direct intervention by a personal God does not remove the intimate spirit of these customs.

Chivalry possessed its own Mysteries. It knew a Temple that did not identify itself purely and simply with the Church of Rome. It produced a literature and cycles of legends in which ancient pre-Christian traditions lived again. The Grail cycle is characteristic because it interweaves the theme of heroic-initiatory reintegration with the mission of restoring a fallen kingdom. It forged a secret language under which a marked hostility to the Roman Curia often hid itself. Even in the great historical knightly orders, which showed a clear tendency to reconstitute the unity of the warrior and the ascetic types, subterranean currents acted. Where they surfaced they attracted the legitimate suspicion and often the persecution of the representatives of the dominant religion. In chivalry there also acted the impulse toward a traditional reconstitution in the highest sense, implying the tacit or explicit surpassing of the Christian religious spirit. The symbolic rite of the rejection of the Cross among the Templars is recalled. All of this had the Empire as its ideal center. Legends arose that took up the theme of the Dry Tree, in which the reflowering of the tree coincides with the intervention of an emperor who will declare war on the clergy, to the point that he is sometimes given the traits of the Antichrist—an obscure expression of the sensation of a spirituality irreducible to Christian spirituality.

The Crusades realized, for the first and last time in post-Roman Europe, on the plane of action and by a marvelous élan, the ideal of the unity of the nations represented in peacetime by the Empire. They appear as a mysterious repetition of the great prehistoric movement of the North and the South, of the West toward the East. Analysis of the deep forces that determined and directed the Crusades does not confirm the views proper to a two-dimensional history. In the current toward Jerusalem there often manifested an occult current against papal Rome that Rome itself, without knowing it, fed. Chivalry was the militia of this current; the heroic-Ghibelline ideal was its most living force. It was to end with an Emperor whom Gregory IX stigmatized as one who threatens to substitute the ancient rites of the pagan peoples for the Christian faith and, by seating himself in the temple, usurps the functions of the priesthood. The figure of Godfrey of Bouillon, that characteristic representative of the crusading chivalry called lux monachorum, is that of a Ghibelline prince. He mounted the throne of Jerusalem only after having carried iron and fire to Rome, after having killed the anti-Caesar Rudolf of Rheinfelden with his own hand, and after having driven the Pope from the holy city. Legend establishes a significant kinship between this king of the crusaders and the mythical swan knight. The swan knight incarnates in turn imperial Roman symbols, solar symbols, and Hyperborean symbols. From these historical and mythic elements it results that, on the plane of the Crusades, Godfrey of Bouillon also represents a symbol of the meaning of that secret force of which one must see, in the political struggle of the Teutonic emperors and even in the victory of Otto I, only an exterior and contingent manifestation.

The knightly ethic and the articulation of the feudal regime, so distant from the social ideal of the Church of the origins; the resuscitated principle of a warrior caste ascetically and sacramentally reintegrated; the secret ideal of the Empire and that of the Crusades—these impose solid limits on Christian influence. The Church accepts them in part: it lets itself be dominated, it romanizes itself in order to be able to dominate and to remain at the crest of the wave. But it resists in part; it wants to undermine the summit and dominate the Empire. The tear remains. The forces evoked escape here and there from the hands of their evokers. Then the two adversaries disengage themselves from one another. The medieval revival is real and dense. It is also incomplete. In the Ghibelline doctrine of Hugh of Fleury the primacy, even sacral, of the Empire is limited to the ordo and excluded from the dignitas, which belongs only to the Church. No medieval monarch, however powerful, felt capable of celebrating the function of the rite and the sacrifice that had passed to the clergy. The Hohenstaufen went far in claiming the supernatural character of the Empire, yet they did not recover for its representative the primordial function of the rex sacrorum. The last luminous flicker of the traditional form in the West occurs here. After it the processes of secularization, the rise of nations, and the further regression of the castes take the field.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is exact. To recognize the revival of the Empire and the Ghibelline Middle Ages is to hold the distinction between the solar-regal principle reasserted by the imperial idea and the lunar-sacerdotal claim that sought to subordinate it. The first measures authority by the capacity to stand as a bridge between the two natures and to order a multiplicity of peoples under a higher measure. The second measures authority by the exclusive possession of spiritual dignitas and by the reduction of the temporal power to a delegated benefit. Without that distinction the medieval world appears only as a Christian civilization, and the precise character of the last great attempt to reconstitute a virile and traditional imperial form in the West remains invisible. The subsequent chapters will examine the decline of this medieval ecumene and the birth of the nations.
