The Two Paths in the Afterlife

“The belief that everybody’s soul is immortal is rather odd; very little evidence of it can be found in the world of Tradition. In Tradition, a distinction was made between true immortality, which corresponded to participation in the Olympian nature of a god, and mere survival.” — Julius Evola, Revolt Against the Modern World

The traditional teaching on what follows death is not the modern belief that every soul is automatically immortal. Tradition distinguished true immortality from mere survival. True immortality is participation in the Olympian nature of a god: a condition that already transcends the human aggregate. Mere survival is the temporary persistence of residual elements that eventually dissolve. Man is not a simple pair of body and soul. Traditional doctrine recognized three essential principles, each with its own character and destiny. The first is the ordinary conscious I of waking life, formed in parallel with the body. The second is the deeper vital force called the demon, the lar, the double, or the totem: the individuating power that lies behind ordinary consciousness and governs organic processes and subtle relations. The third is what proceeds from the first after death: for most men, the shadow. As long as a man remains within nature, his ultimate root is this demon or totem. It is the force that generated the finite consciousness and continues to sustain it from below.

For the ordinary man the path after death is a return to the matrix. The conscious personality, already provisional in life, is lost. What remains is a reduced shadow destined to dissolve after a longer or shorter interval. That interval ends in what several traditions called the second death. The essential vital principles return to the totem, the ancestral force of the stock or race, from which new individual forms will later arise under the same conditions. The cult of the manes, the lares, and the ancestors is the recognition of this fact: the dead continue to live in the collective vital substance of the blood, not as discrete immortal persons. This is the natural order. It is the destiny of those who have remained closed in the lower nature, identified with becoming, and without a principle that can stand outside the cycle of generation and dissolution. The modern assumption that every individual automatically continues as an immortal soul has almost no counterpart in the traditional world. It confuses the residual shadow with the rare possibility of a higher survival.

A second path exists. It is the privileged, aristocratic, and sacral solution. The heroes and demi-gods from whom the higher castes traced their origin did not emit a shadow that would itself die. They had already triumphed over the second death. Through the transformative action of the rite and of the sacrifice they had changed the nature of the vital force itself. In Egypt this was expressed with particular clarity: by the proper ritual operation a new incorruptible body, the sahu, is formed within the substance of the ka (the double) so that it can stand in the invisible in place of the body of flesh. Other traditions speak of the immortal body, the glorious body, or the body of resurrection. In the Homeric age and in the early Vedic period, survival was not imagined as the bare soul floating free; those who were taken by the gods to the Island of the Blessed retained soul and body together in a condition beyond death. This is not a crude materialism. It is the symbolic expression of a principle that has already been integrated and made independent of the lower aggregate. The path is open only to those who, in life, have already won a non-human quality.

The two paths therefore correspond to the two natures already set out in the earlier chapters. The first path belongs to those who remain in the order of becoming and of the demon. Their post-mortem condition is a gradual reabsorption into the ancestral substance that generated them. The second path belongs to those who have established a solar center and who have used the rite, the initiation, or the heroic action to transform the vital force rather than merely ride it. For them death is not the end of the personality but the occasion on which a higher form, already prepared, stands free. The traditional aristocracy and the sacred royalty rested on the claim that their founders had taken this second path and had thereby transmitted a quality that ordinary blood does not carry. Without that claim the distinction between patrician and plebeian, between those who possess the rites and those who do not, loses its ultimate ground. The afterlife is not a separate doctrine. It is the final test of whether the higher principle was actually present in life.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is the refusal of the modern equalizing belief that every soul is immortal by default. Tradition measured the possibility of true survival by the quality already won in the visible world. The ordinary path ends in the second death and the return to the totem. The higher path is the continuation of the solar and virile principle beyond the body. To hold the distinction is to recognize that the structures of hierarchy, caste, and sacred kingship were not social conventions; they were the earthly signs of those who had already secured a different destiny. The absence of that distinction in the present age is the reason the modern world can speak of immortality only as a vague consolation offered to everyone, without the corresponding demand that a higher nature first be realized.
