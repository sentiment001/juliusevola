The Two Paths in the Afterlife

“The belief that everybody’s soul is immortal is rather odd; very little evidence of it can be found in the world of Tradition. In Tradition, a distinction was made between true immortality, which corresponded to participation in the Olympian nature of a god, and mere survival.” — Julius Evola, Revolt Against the Modern World

The traditional teaching on death and what follows it does not coincide with the modern assumption that every human soul is immortal by nature. That assumption is almost without counterpart in the world of Tradition. What Tradition preserved was a sharp distinction between true immortality and mere survival. True immortality is participation in the Olympian nature of a god: a condition that already stands outside the human aggregate of body and ordinary consciousness. Mere survival is the residual persistence of certain elements that eventually dissolve. Man is not composed of only two principles. The older doctrine recognized at least three. The first is the ordinary conscious I formed in parallel with the physical body during waking life. The second is the deeper individuating and vital force known under various names: the demon, the lar, the double, the totem. This force lies behind ordinary consciousness; it governs the organic processes and the subtle relations of the individual, and it is the true root of the finite personality so long as that personality remains within nature. The third principle is what issues from the first after the dissolution of the body: for the great majority of men, the shadow. As long as a man has not established a higher center, his ultimate root is this demon or totem. It is the power that generated the conscious I and that continues to sustain it from below.

For those who have remained in the natural order the path after death is a return. The conscious personality, already provisional and dependent in life, is lost. What remains is a reduced shadow destined to dissolve after a longer or shorter interval. That interval terminates in what several traditions called the second death. The essential vital principles are reabsorbed into the totem, the ancestral force of the stock or race, from which new individual forms will later arise under the same conditions of becoming. The ancient cult of the manes, the lares, and the ancestors is the recognition of this fact: the dead continue to exist in the collective vital substance of the blood, not as discrete immortal persons who retain their earthly individuality. This is the ordinary and natural destiny. It belongs to those who have stayed closed inside the lower nature, identified with the flux of becoming, and without a principle capable of standing outside the cycle of generation and dissolution. The modern belief that every individual automatically continues as an immortal soul confuses the residual shadow with the rare possibility of a higher survival. It equalizes what Tradition ranked.

A second path exists. It is the privileged, aristocratic, and sacral solution reserved for those who have already transformed the vital force while still in the body. The heroes and demi-gods from whom the higher castes traced their origin did not emit a shadow that would itself die. They had already triumphed over the second death. Through the transformative action of the rite, the sacrifice, or the initiation they had changed the nature of the demon itself so that it could support a form independent of the lower aggregate. In Egypt this operation was stated with particular clarity: by the proper ritual procedure a new incorruptible body, the sahu, is formed within the substance of the ka (the double) so that it can stand in the invisible in place of the body of flesh. Other traditions speak of the immortal body, the glorious body, or the body of resurrection. In the Homeric age and in the early Vedic period the higher survival was not imagined as a bare soul floating free of the body; those taken by the gods to the Island of the Blessed or to the equivalent solar abode retained soul and body together in a condition beyond ordinary death. This is not a crude materialism. It is the symbolic expression of a principle that has already been integrated and made independent. The path is open only to those who, in life, have already won a non-human quality.

The two paths therefore correspond directly to the two natures already set out in the chapters on the patriciate and on spiritual virility. The first path belongs to those who remain in the order of becoming and of the demon. Their post-mortem condition is a gradual reabsorption into the ancestral substance that generated them. The second path belongs to those who have established a solar center and who have used the rite, the initiation, or the heroic action to transform the vital force rather than merely ride it. For them death is not the end of the personality but the occasion on which a higher form, already prepared, stands free. The traditional aristocracy and the sacred royalty rested on the claim that their founders had taken this second path and had thereby transmitted a quality that ordinary blood does not carry. Without that claim the distinction between patrician and plebeian, between those who possess the rites and those who do not, loses its ultimate ground. The doctrine of the afterlife is not a separate speculation. It is the final test of whether the higher principle was actually present and active in life.

The same distinction governs the meaning of the ancestral cult and of the transmission of blood. Where the higher path has been secured, the blood itself can carry a non-human quality that ordinary heredity does not transmit. Where it has not been secured, the blood remains the vehicle of the totemic force that will later reabsorb the individual. The modern reduction of the afterlife to a universal consolation offered to every soul erases both the ranking and the demand. It replaces the traditional requirement that a higher nature first be realized with the assumption that survival is automatic. The result is a doctrine that no longer measures men by the quality they have already won and that therefore can no longer ground the hierarchical structures that once rested on that measure.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is the refusal of the equalizing belief that every soul is immortal by default. Tradition measured the possibility of true survival by the quality already established in the visible world. The ordinary path ends in the second death and the return to the totem. The higher path is the continuation of the solar and virile principle beyond the body. To hold the distinction is to recognize that the structures of hierarchy, caste, and sacred kingship were not social conventions; they were the earthly signs of those who had already secured a different destiny. The absence of that distinction in the present age is the reason the modern world can speak of immortality only as a vague consolation offered to everyone, without the corresponding requirement that a higher nature first be realized.
