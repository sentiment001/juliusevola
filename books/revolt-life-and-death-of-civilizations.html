Life and Death of Civilizations

“At the origin of every true civilization there lies a ‘divine’ event (every great civilization has its own myth concerning divine founders): thus, no human or naturalistic factor can fully account for it. The adulteration and decline of civilizations is caused by an event of the same order, though it acts in the opposite, degenerative sense.” — Julius Evola, Revolt Against the Modern World

Where Tradition retains its full force the dynasty or the succession of sacred kings constitutes an axis of light and eternity in time. It is the victorious presence of the superworld in the world, the Olympian component that transfigures the demonic element of the demos and gives a superior meaning to everything that is State, nation, and race. Hierarchical bonds connect individuals to the supernatural. Even where full understanding is lacking, adherence to the traditional norms allows men to find orientation and meaning. Everyday life is transformed into a rite ordered toward liberation. The world of Tradition is not a collection of customs. It is the concrete ordering of the lower by the higher, sustained by the continuous action of a principle that stands outside becoming.

Civilizations, like men, have after a dawn and a development a destiny of decadence and end. The search for the law that presides over this destiny has often fixed on secondary causes. Political disruption, moral decay, economic factors, or external conquest are cited. Arthur de Gobineau showed the insufficiency of such explanations. Civilizations can endure profound turmoil without collapsing so long as the essential spiritual unity remains. The essence of a civilization is not empirical governance or a set of moral considerations. It is the presence of a higher principle that gives form and limit to the lower. When that principle is active the structures hold. When contact with it is lost the structures become empty forms that collapse at the first serious shock.

Some reduce the life and death of civilizations to the purity of blood or race. This view mistakes a condition for the cause. True civilization arises from a supernatural force that transcends physical attributes. The race of the spirit is prior. Purity of blood can support the transmission of a quality already established, but it cannot generate that quality. The loss of contact with the spiritual foundation is the decisive event. Blood alone, once the higher contact is broken, reverts to the totemic force that belongs to the lower nature. The decline is not primarily a biological mixing. It is the withdrawal of the Olympian component that once held the demonic element of the demos in order.

For a civilization to endure its social order must rest on individuals or an elite capable of embodying the spiritual principles that guide the norms. Effective participation in Tradition allows men to grasp their place in the hierarchy intuitively. When spiritual authority becomes merely nominal, when the sacred king or the higher caste no longer actualizes the higher principle, the dam is broken. Ancient grandeur may remain as appearance or curiosity, its esoteric secrets long forgotten. The echoes of the superior forces grow fainter and finally silent. What takes the place of the ordered empire is its inversion: the modern Leviathan, a mechanized and totalitarian collective system in which the demonism of the pure demos and the world of the masses awakens in structures that no longer know a higher measure.

From the distant myth of divine kingship the descent proceeds caste by caste until the faceless forms of the present civilization. The evolution that must be opposed is this progressive reduction of the higher to the lower, the replacement of the axis of light by the regime of quantity and of the mass. The process is not accidental. It follows from the loss of the divine fact that founded the civilization. No human or naturalistic factor can account for the origin, and no such factor can reverse the decline once the higher contact is gone. The ordered world returns to the state in which death and contingency are the supreme rule.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is the recognition that the life and death of civilizations are governed by the same distinction between the two natures that structures the earlier chapters. A true civilization begins with a divine event and is sustained by the continuous presence of the higher principle. Its decline is the corresponding degenerative event: the loss of that presence. Secondary causes are symptoms. Blood is a vehicle, not the source. The modern mechanized mass is the terminal form of the process once the Olympian component has withdrawn. To hold this is to see the present age not as progress but as the completed descent, and to measure any claim of renewal by whether it restores contact with the higher principle or merely rearranges the lower.
