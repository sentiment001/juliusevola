The Cycles of Decadence and the Heroic Cycle

“The heroes who become immortal are those whose adventure succeeds; in other words, they correspond to those who are really capable of overcoming, thanks to an inner impulse toward transcendence, the deviation proper to the Titanic attempt to restore the primordial spiritual virility.” Revolt Against the Modern World

Six fundamental types of civilizations and traditions posterior to the primordial civilization of the Golden Age can be defined. On one side there is Demetrism, which is the purity of the Light of the South (Silver Age, Atlantic cycle, sacerdotal society); Aphroditism, which is its degenerate form; and Amazonism, a deviant attempt at lunar restoration. On the other side there is Titanism or Luciferism, degeneration of the Light of the North (Bronze Age, epoch of warriors and giants); Dionysianism, a masculine aspiration that has become deviant and de-virilized in passive and mixed forms of ecstasy; and finally heroism, as the restoration of the Olympian-solar spirituality and the surpassing of the Mother as well as of the Titan. Upon recognizing the affinity, hidden behind various symbols, of the deeds of the Titans with those of the heroes, one realizes that the hero and the Titans belong after all to the same stock. They are the daring ones who undertook the same transcendent adventure, which can either fail or succeed. The heroes who become immortal are those whose adventure succeeds. They correspond to those who are really capable of overcoming, thanks to an inner impulse toward transcendence, the deviation proper to the Titanic attempt to restore the primordial spiritual virility and the supremacy over the woman, that is, over the lunar spirit, both Aphrodisian and Amazonian. The other heroes, those who are not capable of realizing such a possibility conferred upon them by the Olympian principle, or Zeus, remain in the condition of the failed attempt.

A relevant motif in the cycles of the heroic type is that of the opposition to any gynaecocratic claim and to any Amazonian attempt to usurp power. This motif, together with the previous one which is equally essential for a definition of the notion of hero and refers to an alliance with the Olympian principle and a struggle against the Titanic principle, had a very clear expression in the Hellenic cycle, especially in the figure of the Doric Heracles. Heracles, like Theseus, Bellerophon, and Achilles, fights against the Amazons and eventually exterminates them. Though the Lydian Heracles falls for Omphale, the Doric Heracles proves to be a true misogynist, an enemy of women. Once Prometheus was freed, he taught Heracles the way to reach the Garden of the Hesperides, where he would find the fruit that renders one immortal. The double aspect of the symbolism of Atlas, of Titanic stock, appears here as well: the idea of a polar function and that of a punishment are confounded. Hesiod places the race of the heroes, called demigods, between the Bronze Age and the Iron Age. Many were destroyed in the grim wars at Thebes and Troy, but Zeus removed others to the ends of the earth where they live on the Islands of the Blessed, beside the deep-swirling Ocean, under the rule of Kronos. The heroic cycle is therefore a partial and special restoration of the primordial state within the process of involution.

The cycles of decadence are the successive conditionings of the original Uranian principle by the lunar, the telluric, the Titanic, and the Dionysian. Demetrism and Aphroditism mark the Silver Age. Titanism marks the Bronze Age. Dionysianism is the passive and mixed form of masculine aspiration. Amazonism is the deviant lunar attempt. Heroism is the only form that succeeds in restoring the Olympian-solar spirituality by overcoming both the Mother and the Titan. It is not a return to the pure Golden Age. It is the possibility, conferred by the Olympian principle, of an inner impulse toward transcendence that reestablishes the supremacy of the virile and solar over the lunar and maternal. The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. To recognize the cycles of decadence and the heroic cycle is to hold the distinction between the failed Titanic attempt and the successful heroic restoration, between the lunar conditioning of the Mother and the Olympian surpassing of both Mother and Titan. Without that recognition the later history of the West remains a series of unanchored events, and the revolt against the modern world has no intermediate measure of restoration against which to judge the present darkening.
