On the Primordial Nature of the Patriciate

“The supernatural element was the foundation of the idea of a traditional patriciate and of legitimate royalty: what constituted an ancient aristocrat was not merely a biological legacy or a racial selection, but rather a sacred tradition.” — Julius Evola, Revolt Against the Modern World

The Indo-Aryan order shows the principle in its clearest form. The brahmana caste did not stand at the summit because it held material force, wealth, or an organized ecclesiastical apparatus. Its higher place was fixed by the sacrificial rite that belonged to it alone. The rite and the sacrifice charged those who performed them with a power at once dread and beneficial. Through that charge the brahmana partook of the same nature as the forces he evoked. The quality remained in the man for the rest of his life and made him superior, revered, and feared in his own person. It passed into the blood as a transcendent legacy and became a characteristic of the stock. The rite of initiation later activated it again in the individual. Caste dignity was measured by the difficulty and the usefulness of the functions proper to it. In the traditional world nothing was held more useful than the spiritual influences the rite could set in motion by its necessitating action, and nothing more difficult than entering a real relation with invisible forces that overthrew the unqualified. Scattered across India and possessing no material unity, the brahmana caste still commanded a respect and prestige no armed tyrant ever matched.

The same principle defined the patriciate in China, Greece, and Rome. The patrician stock was marked by possession and exercise of the rites bound to the divine force of the founder. In China only the patricians practiced the rites (yi-li); the plebeians had only customs (su). The Eastern maxim that the rites do not descend to ordinary people corresponds to the Roman saying of Appius Claudius: Auspicia sunt patrum. The plebeians were described as gentem non habent: they have neither rites nor ancestors. To the Roman patrician the plebeian manner of life and of sexual union appeared little different from that of wild animals. The supernatural element therefore stood at the base of the traditional idea of the patriciate exactly as it stood at the base of legitimate royalty. What made the ancient aristocrat was a sacred tradition, not a mere biological legacy or a racial selection. An animal can possess biological purity and integrity of blood. The laws of heredity and endogamy applied to the other castes as well. The true distinction was that the ancestors of the plebeian and of the slave were not divine ancestors (divi parentes). No transcendent quality or form, held under a rigorous and secret ritual tradition, passed through their blood. They lacked the power by which the aristocracy could celebrate its own cult and itself form the priestly class. They lacked the second birth that marked the arya; the Manavadharmasastra states that even an arya is not superior to the sudra until he has been born again. They were not purified by the three heavenly fires that in ancient Iran stood as the occult souls of the three higher classes of the empire. They lacked the solar element that marked the race of the Incas. Their promiscuity knew no limit. They had no true cult of their own and, in the higher sense, no father (patrem ciere non possunt). Their religion could only be collective and chthonic: frenzied and ecstatic forms tied to pre-Aryan substrata in India, the cult of the mothers and of subterranean forces in the Mediterranean, the practices of the “obsessed” and of shamanic popular cults in China.

The same supernatural conception of the aristocracy appears among the ancient Teutons. Every leader was at the same time high priest of his people and of his land. The claim of a divine ancestor was enough to set a family apart from all others, and kings were chosen only from those families. The king therefore held a dignity distinct from that of a military leader (dux or heritzogo) appointed for a particular campaign on the basis of individual talent. Ancient Norwegian kings performed the rites themselves, without a separate priestly class. Even among so-called primitive populations the uninitiated were treated as the barbarians of their own society and were excluded from the military and political privileges of the clan. Before the rites that were meant to transform a man’s innermost nature, often accompanied by hard trials and a period of isolation, a person was not regarded as a true man; he belonged with women, children, and animals. Only through the new life awakened by initiation, a life almost unrelated to the old one and accompanied by a new name, a new language, and new attributions, did he enter the group of true men who held the community. Authors such as H. Schurtz rightly saw in this the germ of true political unity. It confirms that the plane of any traditional state differs from the plane of any unity built on merely naturalistic premises. The virile groups (Männerbünde) admitted after a regeneration that truly confers manhood and differentiates the man from every other member of the community hold power (imperium) and an undisputed prestige.

Only in recent times has the idea of aristocracy, like the idea of royalty, taken on a merely secular and political character. At first it rested on character, race, honor, valor, and faithfulness, on noblesse d’épée and noblesse de coeur. Later a plebeian conception of aristocracy arose that denied even the privileges of blood and tradition. A typical product of that conception is the so-called aristocracy of culture or of intellectuals that appeared as a by-product of bourgeois civilization. In a normal hierarchical view the principle that established precise ontological differences between men, and that grounded the privileges of aristocracy, was never intellectuality but spirituality. The ritual and sacral element likewise founded the authority of the higher castes and of the father in the ancient patrician family. In Western Aryan societies the paterfamilias originally held a status comparable to that of the priest-king. The term pater was synonymous with king and conveyed spiritual authority together with power and majestic dignity. The state itself is the application on a larger scale of the principle that first constituted the patrician family. The pater was military leader and lord of justice, yet first of all he was the one entrusted with the traditional rites and sacrifices proper to the family, the non-human legacy that emanated from the founding father. That legacy was represented by the fire (the thirty fires of the thirty families surrounding the central fire of Vesta). The father was the virile priest who tended the sacred fire; he appeared to his household as a hero, as the natural mediator of every efficacious relation with the supernatural, as the supreme vivifier of the mystical force present in the fire, as the incarnation of order. His principal duty was to prevent the fire from going out so that it might continue to reproduce and nourish the mystical victory of the ancestor. From this center flowed the rigorous constitution of traditional paternal right, including the ius vitae necisque. In this spirit the articulations of what Vico called the natural heroic rights or the divine rights of heroic peoples were formed.

What united the members of a family was something more powerful than birth, feeling, or physical strength: the cult of the hearth and of the ancestors. The ancient family was more a religious than a natural association. The common rite constituted the true bond of the family and often of the gens itself. An outsider allowed to participate in the rite became an adoptive son and could enjoy privileges that could be taken from a biological son who neglected the rite. Rite rather than blood held the power to unite or to differentiate. A woman joined her husband’s stock through the rite; before she was the bride of a man she was the bride of Agni or of the mystical fire. Those who participated in the cult of a patrician stock received an ennobling mystical participation and committed their offspring to it. The same idea appears in the sacred aspect of the feudal principle, in which the king’s mystical gift of life elevated a body of faithful subjects to priestly dignity. In India the firstborn, who alone has the right to invoke Indra, is the one whose birth frees the father of his debt to the ancestors; he is said to free or save (trayate) the ancestors in the world beyond. He stands on the battlefield of earthly existence and continues the line of influence that constitutes the ancestors’ substance and that is carried in the blood as a purifying fire. In some cases the family itself may have arisen by adaptation from an older, purely spiritual type of unity. Several traditions still preserve the priority of spiritual paternity over natural paternity and of the second birth over natural birth. The birth a teacher produces is real, free from old age and death. Natural relationships are secondary and can even be reversed. The absolute character of patria potestas possessed its spiritual justification only because it rested on this order of spiritual paternity, related to blood ties as the soul is related to the body.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is the recognition that true hierarchy rests on the presence or absence of a higher principle transmitted through a sacred tradition, not on blood, force, wealth, or culture. The modern alternatives reduce aristocracy to a secular or intellectual elite and thereby erase the distinction that once separated those who possessed the rites and the divine ancestors from those who did not. Without that distinction the structures of family, caste, and state lose their non-human foundation and become merely naturalistic aggregates. To hold the idea is to measure every claim to rank by whether it carries the ritual element, the second birth, and the orientation toward the higher nature, or whether it remains closed in the lower order of becoming. The absence of that element is the decisive fact that separates the traditional world from the age that has replaced it.
