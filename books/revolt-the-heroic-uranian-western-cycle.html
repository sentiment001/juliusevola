The Heroic-Uranian Western Cycle

“The Olympian conception of the divine was one of the most characteristic expressions of the Northern Light among the Hellenes; it was the view of a symbolical world of immortal and luminous essences detached from the inferior region of earthly beings and of things subjected to becoming.” Revolt Against the Modern World

When the Western world is examined in its Hellenic form, two aspects appear at once. The first is the persistence of a world still ordered by the general principle of the sacred, in which higher and lower natures are still recognized and in which life is still measured by a vertical standard. The second is the presence of processes that already anticipate the later humanistic, secular, and rationalistic cycle. Because of this second aspect many moderns treat Greece as the origin of their own civilization. The first aspect belongs to the heroic-Uranian Western cycle. The second already points beyond it. Hellenic civilization rests on an older Aegean and Pelasgic substratum. In that substratum the theme of the Atlantic civilization of the Silver Age recurs, especially in its Demetrian form, together with frequent accretions of chthonic and demonic cults. Over against this substratum stand the forms created by the conquering Achaeans and Doric stocks: the Olympian ideal of the Homeric cycle and the cult of the Hyperborean Apollo. The myth of Apollo’s victory over the serpent Python, portrayed beneath the temple at Delphi, carries a double meaning. It describes both a metaphysical event and the struggle between a race that practiced a Uranian cult and a race that practiced a chthonic cult. The reemergence of the earlier stratum later produced varieties of Dionysism, Aphroditism, and other spiritualities tied to the chthonic cult and rite, with their corresponding social and moral forms.

The same stratification appears on the ethnic plane. Three strata can be distinguished. The first is linked to remnants of races entirely foreign to the northwestern or Atlantic cycle and therefore foreign to the Indo-European stocks. The second derives from branches of the Western-Atlantic race that reached the Mediterranean basin in archaic times; this may be called paleo-Indo-European, provided the alteration and involution it underwent are kept in view. The Pelasgic civilization belongs essentially to this stratum. The third stratum corresponds to the Hellenic populations of northwestern origin who entered Greece in relatively recent times. This triple stratification and the dynamic of the corresponding influences also appear in the Italic civilization. In Hellas the stratification may be reflected in the three classes of the Spartiates, Perioeci, and Helots. The reduction from the traditional fourfold division to a threefold one is explained by the presence of an aristocracy that combined warrior and sacred character, as in the stock of the Heracleids or the Geleonts, the Radiant Ones, who claimed Zeus or Geleon as symbolic forefather. Even modern research, setting aside the hostile accounts of the Greek historians, has acknowledged the heterogeneity of the Achaean world relative to the earlier Pelasgic civilization and has noted the affinities in race, custom, and type of civilization between the Achaeans and Dorians on one side and the Northern-Aryan stocks of the Celts, Germanic tribes, Scandinavians, and the Aryans of India on the other. The forces that clashed are visible in the contrast between the plain linear purity, geometric and solar clarity, essentialism, and simplicity of the Doric style, which signified liberation, power, and primordial form, and the chaotic organicism and animal and vegetal symbols of the Cretan-Minoan traces; and in the contrast between the luminous Olympian figurations and the traditions of god-snakes, man-serpents, demons with animal heads, and the magical cult of subterranean fire or of the deities of the waters.

One of the stories of prehistoric Greece is the demise of the legendary kingdom of Minos. In that Pelasgic land Zeus was regarded as a chthonic demon and therefore as a mortal being; the black Mother Earth was the greatest of the deities; the predominant cult, still tied to the feminine element, was that of Hera, Hestia, Themis, and the Nereids; and the supreme limit was the Demetrian lunar mystery with its gynaecocratic transpositions in rite and custom. A trace of the victory of the new civilization appears in Aeschylus’s Eumenides. In the assembly of the gods that judges Orestes for the killing of his mother Clytemnestra, the conflict between the rights of the father and the rights of the mother is set out with precision. Apollo and Athena stand against the nocturnal female deities, the Erinyes, who demand revenge. The declaration that it is possible to be a father without a mother, recalling the symbolic birth of Athena, emphasizes the superior ideal of virility and a spiritual genesis free from the naturalistic plane in which the Mother’s right rules. The absolution of Orestes marks the triumph of a new law, a new custom, a new cult, and a new right. The Erinyes, chthonic female deities with snakes’ heads, daughters of Night, are the symbols of the ancient pre-Hellenic era. It is significant that the judgment takes place on the hill sacred to the warrior god Ares, in the ancient citadel of the Amazons who were overcome by Theseus. The Olympian conception of the divine is one of the most characteristic expressions of the Northern Light among the Hellenes. It is the view of a symbolic world of immortal and luminous essences detached from the inferior region of earthly beings and of things subjected to becoming. The ideas of Chaos as primordial principle, of Night and Erebus as generators of further generation, of the Earth as universal Mother preceding her heavenly groom, and of the contingency of chaotic becoming attributed even to divine natures, are not Hellenic. They are themes that betray the Pelasgic substratum in Hesiod’s syncretism.

Ancient Hellas knew both the Olympian theme and the heroic theme. The heroes were regarded as above mortal and human nature, demigods who participated in the same Olympian immortality. The Dorian and Achaean hero was in many cases defined and forged by action rather than by blood ties with the stock of the gods, that is, by an inherited supernatural status. His being was entirely epic. It did not know the mystical abandonments of the Southern Light nor the return to the generating cosmic womb. Victory crowns the Doric Heracles in the Olympian dwelling characterized by a pure virility immune from the Titanic element. The ideal type was not Prometheus, whom the Hellenes regarded as a vanquished foe of Zeus, but Heracles, the antigynaecocratic hero who triumphed over the Titanic element, freed Prometheus after siding with the Olympian gods, and overcame the forces that belonged to the earlier substratum. The same heroic-Uranian principle that appears in the Hellenic cycle finds its most rigorous and enduring Western expression in Rome. Behind the greatness of Rome stand the forces of the heroic Aryan-Western cycle. The Roman civilization and the Roman man bear witness to the presence and power of the same force that stood at the center of the heroic Uranian cycles of Northern and Western origin. The type of the Roman man, the structure of the patriciate, the sacred character of authority, the conception of the empire as form, and the capacity to hold a higher measure under pressure are the concrete historical expressions of this cycle in the West. The duality found in the couple Romulus and Remus is itself a characteristic theme of the heroic cycles: the two brothers generated from the intercourse between a virgin who tended the sacred fire and the warrior god Mars. In this form the Western cycle carries forward the possibility of a spiritual virility that does not collapse into the lunar or the telluric and that does not exhaust itself in the Titanic attempt.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. To recognize the heroic-Uranian Western cycle is to hold the distinction between the Olympian and heroic principle that entered Greece with the Achaeans and Dorians and that found its most complete Western form in Rome, and the Pelasgic, Demetrian, and chthonic substratum against which it had to establish itself. The first measures existence by luminous, immortal, and virile standards and forges the higher type through action under a higher measure. The second remains bound to the Mother, to the subterranean, and to the naturalistic plane. Without that distinction the Hellenic and Roman worlds appear only as the origins of later humanistic and secular developments, and the specifically Western possibility of a heroic restoration of the Northern Light is lost. The subsequent chapters will examine the syncope of this cycle and the particular inversions through which the antitraditional spirit established itself in the West.
