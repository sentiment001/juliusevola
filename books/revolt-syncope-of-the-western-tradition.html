Syncope of the Western Tradition

“Rome witnessed the return of the symbols of the Mother and of the most spurious forms of the various mystical and pantheistic cults of Southern deities, which were a far cry from the Demetrian clarity of the origins and were associated with the corruption of the customs and of the innermost Roman virtus more than with the corruption of the institutions.” Revolt Against the Modern World

The heroic-Uranian Western cycle that found its clearest historical forms in the Olympian Hellenic world and in Rome did not pass continuously into the later European order. It suffered a syncope, an interruption of continuity in which the higher principle temporarily ceased to act as the effective center of the civilization. This interruption is marked by the exhaustion of the Roman cycle and by the advent of primitive Christianity. Rome had emancipated itself from its earlier Atlantic and Etruscan-Pelasgian roots, destroyed the great centers of the more recent Southern civilization, despised Greek philosophers, banned the Pythagorean sect, and outlawed the Bacchanalia. That same Rome, sacral, patrician, and virile, ordered by the notions of ius, fas, and mos, fell under the onslaught of Asiatic cults. These cults brought the return of the symbols of the Mother and of the most spurious forms of the mystical and pantheistic cults of Southern deities. They were far from the Demetrian clarity of the origins. They corrupted the customs and the innermost Roman virtus more than they corrupted the institutions. The sacred content of the imperial idea was preserved, but only as a mere symbol carried by a turbid and chaotic current.

Primitive Christianity took up several themes typical of the Semitic soul at large. By regarding Jesus as Saviour and by breaking away from the Law, that is, from Jewish orthodoxy, it adopted themes proper to an innerly divided human type. Through Paul’s theology these elements were universalized and activated without a direct relationship to their Jewish origins. They constituted fertile ground for the growth of an anti-traditional virus, especially vis-à-vis a tradition like the Roman one. Christianity modeled itself after a broken human type. It appealed to the irrational part of being. Instead of the paths of heroic, sapiential, and initiatory spiritual growth, it posited faith as its fundamental instrument, the elan of a restless and perturbed soul that is attracted to the supernatural in a confused way. In regard to man, what emerged were the notion of the creature separated by an essential distance from God as its Creator and as a personal, distinct being, and the exasperation of this distance through the revival and the accentuation of the idea of original sin. These themes were of Jewish origins as well. The result was the triumph of pathos over ethos.

Over and against a particular universalism, a new, opposite universalism based on a metaphysical dualism affirmed itself. The traditional hierarchical view according to which loyalty enjoyed a supernatural sanction and a religious value, since every power descended from above, was undermined at its very foundation. In this sinful world there can only be room for a civitas diaboli. The civitas dei, or the divine state, was thought to belong to a separate plane and to consist in the unity of those who are drawn to the otherworld by a confused longing and who, as Christians, acknowledge only Christ as their leader as they await the Last Day. The interruption was not total. Residual elements of the earlier cycle continued to exist. Certain rites, certain conceptions of sacred kingship, and certain heroic attitudes survived in attenuated form. Yet the effective center had shifted. The traditional Western world no longer possessed a living axis capable of ordering the whole of life according to the solar and heroic measure. What had been a civilization oriented by the higher nature now carried within itself a principle that measured existence by the lower nature and by the promise of a salvation that bypassed the need for qualitative differentiation.

Later centuries would see attempts to recover the interrupted continuity. The Ghibelline Middle Ages and the imperial idea would reassert the regal and solar principle against the sacerdotal claim. Certain orders and certain forms of chivalry would carry forward residual elements of the heroic path. These recoveries remain partial. They operate within a world already marked by the syncope, and they never fully restore the pre-Christian Western axis. The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. To recognize the syncope of the Western tradition is to hold the distinction between the heroic-Uranian cycle that ordered the Hellenic and Roman worlds and the dualistic, anti-hierarchical interruption introduced by primitive Christianity. The first measures man by the capacity to stand under a higher law and to act as a bridge between the two natures. The second measures man by humility, equality, and dependence on a grace that does not require qualitative transformation. Without that distinction the later history of the West appears only as a continuous development, and the precise point at which the traditional Western form was suspended remains invisible. The subsequent chapters will examine the attempts at recovery and the further processes through which the modern world completed itself.
