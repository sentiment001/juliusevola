The Doctrine of the Four Ages

“In all the ancient testimonies of traditional humanity one can always find, in one form or another, the idea of a regress, of a fall: from superior original states beings would have descended into states ever more conditioned by the human, mortal and contingent element. The Eddic term ragna-ròkkr, ‘darkening of the gods,’ is that which best characterizes it.” Revolt Against the Modern World

If modern man until yesterday conceived and exalted as an evolution the sense of the history known to him, the truth known to traditional man was the opposite. In all the ancient testimonies of traditional humanity one can always find, in one form or another, the idea of a regress, of a fall: from superior original states beings would have descended into states ever more conditioned by the human, mortal and contingent element. Such an involutive process would have taken its start in remote times, and the Eddic term ragna-ròkkr, “darkening of the gods,” is that which best characterizes it. Nor is it a teaching that in the traditional world remained in a vague and generic form. It defined itself instead in an organic doctrine, itself found with a wide margin of uniformity: the doctrine of the four ages. A process of gradual decadence through four cycles or “generations”: such is, traditionally, the effective sense of history, and therefore also that of the genesis of what, in universal, we have called the “modern world.” This doctrine can therefore serve as the basis for what follows.

The best-known form of the doctrine of the four ages is that proper to the Greco-Roman tradition. Hesiod speaks precisely of four eras, marked by the metals gold, silver, bronze and iron, inserting then between the last two a fifth era, the era of the “heroes,” which however will be seen to have only the significance of a partial and special restoration of the primordial state. The Hindu tradition has the same doctrine in the form of four cycles called respectively satya-yuga (or krita-yuga), treta-yuga, dvapara-yuga and kali-yuga (that is, “dark age”), together with the image of the coming less, in each of them, of each of the four feet or supports of the bull symbolizing the dharma, the traditional law. The Iranian redaction is akin to the Hellenic: the four ages are known and marked by gold, silver, steel and “mixture of iron.” The Chaldean teaching takes up such a view almost in the same terms. In particular, more recently one encounters the image of the chariot of the universe as a quadriga that, conducted by the supreme god, is transported in a circular race by four horses representing the elements: the four ages correspond to the successive prevalence of each of such horses, which then draws the others with it, according to the symbolic nature, more or less luminous and rapid, of the element of which it is the figuration. For as much as in a special transposition, the same conception reappears in the Jewish tradition, in the prophetism speaking of a shining statue, whose head is of gold, whose breast and whose arms are of silver, the belly and the thighs of copper, and the legs and the feet of iron and clay: a statue that represents, in the various parts thus divided, four “kingdoms” that succeed one another starting from the golden one of the “king of kings” who has received “from the god of heaven power, strength and glory.” If for Egypt one already knows of the tradition referred by Eusebius concerning three distinct dynasties, formed respectively by gods, demigods and men, in this one can have the equivalent of the first three ages—from that of gold to that of bronze—of the above. Likewise, if the ancient Aztec traditions speak of five suns or solar cycles, of which the first four correspond to the elements and in which, as in the Euro-Asiatic traditions, figure the catastrophes of fire and of water (deluge) and those struggles against the giants that we shall see characterize the cycle of the “heroes” added by Hesiod to the other four, in this one can equally recognize a variant of the same teaching of which, moreover, in other forms, more or less fragmentarily, one can also find the memory among other peoples.

The traditional teaching is rich in references to the mixture of the “divine” race with the human race in the strict sense, conceived as an inferior race, so much so that in certain texts the “guilt” is compared to sodomy, to the carnal conjunction with the beasts. On one side there is the myth of the Ben-Elohim, or “sons of the gods,” who united with the “daughters of men,” making it so that in the end “every flesh has corrupted its way upon the earth.” On the other there is the Platonic myth of the Atlanteans, conceived likewise as descendants and disciples of the gods, who for their repeated uniting with the humans lose the divine element and end by letting the human nature predominate in them. For relatively more recent epochs the tradition, in its myths, is rich in references to civilizing races and to struggles between divine races and animalistic, cyclopean or demonic races. They are the Aesir in struggle against the elemental beings; they are the Olympians and the “Heroes” in struggle against giants and monsters of the night, of the earth or of the water; they are the Aryan Devas risen against the Asuras, “enemies of the divine heroes”; they are the Incas, the dominators who impose their solar law on the aborigines of the “Mother Earth”; they are the Tuatha de Danann who according to the legendary history of Ireland affirmed themselves against the monstrous races of the Fomorians, and so on. On such a basis one can also say that if the traditional teaching recalls—as substrate anterior to the civilizations created by superior races—stocks that could also correspond to the animalistic and inferior types of evolutionism, evolutionism is however characterized by the error of considering such animalistic stocks as original in the absolute, while they are so only relatively, and of conceiving as forms of “evolution” forms of crossing that presuppose the appearance of other races, superior biologically and as civilization, come from seats of their own, races that either for the remote antiquity (as is the case for that “Hyperborean” or for that “Atlantean”), or for geophysical factors, left only traces hard to rediscover by whoever bases himself on the sole archaeological and paleontological testimonies accessible to profane research.

On the other side, it is very significant the fact that the surviving populations where still the presumed original primitivistic and barbaric state would hold little comfort the evolutionistic hypothesis. They are stocks that, instead of evolving, tend to extinguish themselves, with which they demonstrate to be precisely degenerating residues of cycles whose vital possibilities were exhausted, or else heterogeneous elements, trunks left behind by the central current of humanity. This already holds for Neanderthal man, who in his extreme morphological brutality seems to approach the “ape-man.” Neanderthal man disappeared mysteriously in a given period, and the races that appeared after it—Aurignacian man and above all Cro-Magnon man—and that present a superior type, so much so that one can already recognize in them the stock of many of the present human races, cannot be considered as “evolutionary forms” of Neanderthal man. The same holds for the Grimaldi race, also extinct. The same can be said for many “savage” peoples still living: they do not “evolve,” they extinguish themselves. Their “civilizing” is not an “evolution,” but almost always a brusque mutation that strikes their vital possibilities. In fact, for the possibility of evolving or of decaying there exist given limits. There are species that conserve their characteristics even near conditions relatively different from those natural to them; others, instead, in such case extinguish themselves; or else mixtures intervene with other elements, in which, in the end, one has neither assimilation nor true evolution. For the result of these mixtures something similar rather holds to the processes considered by Mendel’s laws on heredity: disappeared in the phenotype, the primitivistic element maintains itself as a latent separate inheritance capable of re-sprouting in sporadic appearances, however always with character of heterogeneity with respect to the superior type. The evolutionists believe they hold themselves “positively” to the facts. They do not doubt that the facts, in themselves, are mute; that the same facts, interpreted variously, give testimony for the most various theses. Thus it has happened that someone, while having in view all the data adduced as proofs by the theory of evolution, has shown that they, in last analysis, could also comfort the contrary thesis—which, under more than one regard, corresponds to the traditional teaching: the thesis, that is, that, far from man being a product of “evolution” of the animal species, many animal species are to be considered as lateral trunks in which a primordial impulse has aborted, having only in the superior human races its direct and adequate manifestation. There are ancient myths of divine stirps in struggle against monstrous entities or animalistic demons before the establishing of the race of the mortals (that is, of humanity in its most recent form), which, among other things, could refer precisely to the struggle of the primordial human principle against the animal potentialities that were to be overcome or left behind.

In the prehistoric caves (many of which betray a sacral orientation) the “primitive” man did not have his bestial dwellings, but the places of a cult, remained in such form also in epochs indubitably “civilized” (for example, the Greco-Minoan cult of the caves, the ceremonies and the initiatory retreats on Ida); and that it is natural to find only there, for the natural protection of the place, traces that elsewhere time, men and the elements could not likewise let arrive to our contemporaries. In general, it is a basal traditional idea that the state of knowledge and of civilization was the natural state, if not of man in general, at least of determinate elites of the origins; that knowledge was as little “constructed” and acquired as little the true sovereignty had origin from below. Joseph de Maistre, after having put in light that what a Rousseau and his similars had presumed to be the state of nature (with reference to the savages) is only the last degree of brutalization of some dispersed stocks or taken by the consequences of some degradation or prevarication that struck their deepest substance, quite rightly says: “About the path of science we are blinded by a coarse sophism, which has bewitched every glance: it is the judging of the times in which men saw the effects in the causes, on the basis of the times in which they laboriously reascend from the effects to the causes, in which indeed one occupies oneself only with the effects, in which one says that it is useless to occupy oneself with the causes, in which one no longer knows what a cause means.” At the beginning one possessed not only a science, but “a science different from ours, which took its start on high, which rendered it even most dangerous. This explains why science at the beginnings was always mysterious and enclosed in the temples, where it extinguished itself in the end, when this flame could no longer serve if not to burn.” And it is then that, little by little, as surrogate, the other science began to form, that purely human and empirical, of which the moderns are so proud and with which they have thought to measure all that, for them, is civilization. This last, on such a basis, has only the significance of a vain attempt to re-lift itself, by means of surrogates, from an unnatural, by no means original, state of degradation, no longer even perceived as such. In any case, one must realize that these and similar indications little can avail for whoever is not disposed to change his own mentality. Every epoch has its “myth,” which reflects a determinate collective climate. That, in general, to the aristocratic idea of the coming from on high, of the having a past of light and of spirit, today has been substituted the democratic idea of evolutionism, which makes the superior derive from the inferior, man from the animal, civilization from barbarism: in this one has much less the “objective” result of a conscious and free scientific inquiry, than one of the many reflexes that by underground ways the advent of the modern world of the inferior strata, of the man without tradition, has necessarily produced on the intellectual and cultural plane. Thus there is no need to delude oneself: some “positive” superstitions will always have way to create for themselves alibis to defend themselves. Not so much new “facts” will be able to lead to the recognition of different horizons, as a new attitude before them. And every attempt to valorize also from the scientific point of view what here one intends to expose above all from the traditional dogmatic point of view will be able to have results only among those who are already spiritually predisposed to welcome knowledges of the kind.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. History is not ascent from the lower to the higher. It is the progressive conditioning of an original superior state by the human, mortal and contingent element, marked by the four ages and culminating in the darkening that defines the present cycle. The myths of mixture and of struggle between higher and lower races, the extinction of residual stocks, the sacral character of the earliest traces, and the aristocratic origin of true knowledge all converge on the same point: the modern world is the terminal phase of an involution, not the product of an evolution. To hold the doctrine of the four ages is to refuse the democratic myth that makes the superior derive from the inferior and to orient oneself by the higher original measure. Without that recognition the genesis of the modern world remains unintelligible, and the revolt against it has no ground.
