Universality and Centralism

“The ideal of the Holy Roman Empire is the one that, by contrast, best brings into evidence the decadence suffered by the principle of ‘reigning’ when it loses its spiritual basis. The Ghibelline ideal of the Holy Roman Empire implied, in the clearest possible way, both the sense that the Regnum has a supernatural origin and a suprapolitical and universal nature, and the sense that the emperor, as lex animata in terris and vertex of the ordinatio ad unum, is aliquod unum quod non est pars (Dante).” Revolt Against the Modern World

The Empire is not a larger kingdom. It is a qualitatively different order. The emperor stands as the living law on earth and as the summit of the ordering toward unity. He is one that is not a part. The Empire itself is prior and superior to the particular kingdoms and nations it encompasses. Historians who treat the medieval contrast between the emperor’s absolute, ecumenical right and the limited material reach of his power as an inconsistency miss the point. The plane of a true universal function is not the plane of matter. Unity that claims only material, political, or military force cannot fulfill the function. The bond that held the kingdoms to the Empire was the ideal and spiritual bond named fides. In the medieval language the term carried both a religious sense and the politico-moral sense of fidelity or devotion. Raised to the dignity of a sacrament, the sacramentum fidelitatis, it was the principle of all honor and the cement of the feudal communities. Fidelity linked the vassal to his prince. In a higher, purified, and immaterial form it linked the partial units to the center of gravity of the Empire, a transcendent power that, in principle, did not need to take up arms in order to be recognized.

Unity and hierarchy therefore coexisted with a large margin of independence, freedom, and articulation. In the properly Aryan civilizations a long period existed in which free pluralism prevailed inside each state or city. Families, lineages, and gentes appeared as so many miniature states, largely autonomous powers that possessed what was necessary for material and spiritual life: a cult, a law, a land, a militia. Tradition and the community of origin and of race (race not merely of the body but also of the spirit) formed the base of the higher organization. That organization could expand to the form of Empire when the original group of forces radiated into a wider space that required ordering and unification. The early Frankish period is significant. “Frank” was synonymous with free man, bearer by race of a dignity that raised him above all other men. Until the ninth century the community of civilization and membership in the Frankish stock constituted the state without any politically organized and centralized unity coextensive with a national territory in the modern sense. Later, during the Carolingian development and the constitution of the Empire, the Frankish nobility was scattered everywhere. These detached, extremely autonomous units that nonetheless maintained an immaterial link with the center functioned as the vital unifying element of the whole, like the cells of the nervous system in the organism.

The extreme-Oriental tradition stressed the same limit-idea of true unity and true authority. By detaching itself from the peripheral domain, by not intervening directly, by remaining in the essential immateriality of the center (comparable to the hub of a wheel that conditions the entire movement), the center attains the “virtue” that defines the true empire. Individuals retain the sensation of being free. Everything unfolds in order because, thanks to the reciprocal compensation produced by the invisible direction, partial disorders or arbitrary acts only contribute to the total order. When the opposite idea is affirmed, when sovereignty and unity dominate multiplicity only in a material, direct, and political manner, intervening everywhere, abolishing every autonomy of particular groups, leveling all rights and privileges in an absolutist spirit, denaturing and oppressing the various ethnic stocks, then true imperiality disappears. One is no longer in the presence of an organism but of a mechanism. That is the type of the modern national and centralizing states. Wherever a monarch descended to this plane, wherever he, falling from his spiritual function, promoted a politico-material absolutism and centralization, emancipating himself from every bond toward sacred authority, humiliating the feudal nobility, seizing the powers previously distributed among the aristocracy, he dug his own grave and attracted a fatal reaction. Absolutism is only a brief mirage. Leveling prepares demagogy, the ascent of the people, of the demos, to the profaned throne. This is the case of the tyranny that succeeded the previous aristocratic-sacred regime in many Greek cities. It is, to a certain extent, the case of Rome and Byzantium in the leveling forms of imperial decadence. It is, finally, the sense of European political history after the collapse of the spiritual ideal of the Holy Roman Empire and the subsequent constitution of secularized national monarchies, down to the terminal phenomenon of “totalitarianism.”

Of the great powers born from the hypertrophy of nationalism and from a barbaric will to power of a military or economic type, to which the name of empires continued to be given, it is scarcely worth speaking. An Empire exists only by virtue of superior values to which a given race has raised itself, first by surpassing itself, by surpassing its natural particularities. That race then becomes the highest bearer of a principle that exists equally, yet only in potential form, among other peoples that possess, to some degree, a traditional organization. In such a case the material action of conquest appears as an action that overthrows empirical barriers and raises the various potentialities to a single level of action, thereby producing a unification in the real sense of the word. “Die and become,” as in a being struck by the “ray of Apollo,” is the elementary premise for any race that aspires to an imperial mission and dignity. This is precisely the opposite of the morality that corresponds to what is called the “sacred egoism” of nations. Modern imperialism that remains enclosed in the particularity of a people or a nation aborts by the very absence of the supranational spiritual element. Violence replaces the higher principle and acts upon the living organism like a cancerous growth.

The demand the chapter places on the reader is therefore exact. Authority that seeks unity by material force, by the abolition of difference, or by the leveling of particular stocks has already abandoned the traditional measure. The true center remains immaterial. It commands without constraining. It integrates difference under a higher principle rather than abolishing it. When that center is lost, the forms that claim the name of empire become mechanisms of domination. The recognition of the difference between the organic Empire and the mechanical state is the condition for seeing why the Ghibelline ideal still carried a residual force that later national and totalitarian constructions no longer possess.
