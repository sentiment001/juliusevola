Day 27 — Rootlessness

"The modern man is characteristically a rootless being. He has lost contact with the soil, with the blood, with the tradition, and with any form of belonging that is more than contractual and temporary." — Julius Evola, Men Among the Ruins

Rootlessness is the condition of a man who no longer stands within any organic continuity. Family, place, ancestral memory, and the inherited forms of a people once provided a natural framework that shaped the individual before he began to choose. In the modern environment those frameworks have been weakened or deliberately dismantled. The result is a type who must invent his own identity from the materials of the moment.

This condition is often praised as liberation. The young man is told that he is free to become anything precisely because nothing binds him in advance. The cost of that freedom is the loss of depth. A life without roots tends toward surface intensity and rapid exhaustion. Belonging becomes a consumer choice rather than a given that forms character.

Evola’s diagnosis does not call for a sentimental return to an idealized past. It calls for the recognition that a man without any interior continuity is more easily shaped by the forces of the age. The rootless individual is available. He can be moved by fashion, by ideology, and by the successive waves of collective emotion because nothing in him stands firm against them.

The work of differentiation includes the recovery of a sense of rootedness that is first interior. Even when external forms are broken, the man can still orient himself by a standard that does not shift with the market or the media. That interior rootedness is the beginning of a real resistance to the rootless condition of the age.
